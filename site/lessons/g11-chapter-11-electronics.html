11.1  Conductors, insulators and semiconductors

Electronic devices such as phones, computers, televisions and radios are built from special materials. Conductors (copper, aluminium, iron, lead; alloys such as brass, nichrome, manganin) have electrons in the outer shell that are loosely held and move freely. Insulators (ebonite, polythene, plastic, dry wood, asbestos, glass) have strong bonds, so almost no electrons are free. Semiconductors conduct a little.

Silicon (Si) and germanium (Ge), in crystal form, are semiconductors. They are in group IV and have four outer electrons. Each atom shares them with four neighbours in covalent bonds, giving eight outer electrons: a stable crystal lattice.

Figure 11.2: a silicon lattice at 0 K.
Figure 11.3: above 0 K, heat breaks some bonds.

These bonds are weak: even at room temperature the thermal energy breaks some, freeing electrons. Each freed electron leaves a hole, a missing electron that acts like a positive charge. An electron from a neighbouring atom can jump into the hole, so the hole moves too. In a semiconductor both electrons (negative carriers) and holes (positive carriers) carry current; their numbers are equal, so the crystal stays neutral. In a metal only electrons carry the current.

🌡️ Effect of temperature
  • Conductors: higher temperature means more random motion of the free electrons, which obstructs the current: conductivity decreases (resistivity increases).
  • Semiconductors: higher temperature breaks more bonds, giving more holes and electrons: conductivity increases (resistivity decreases).

11.2  Doping: n-type and p-type semiconductors

Pure silicon or germanium is an intrinsic semiconductor. Adding (doping) a tiny amount of another element makes an extrinsic semiconductor with many more carriers.

Figure 11.4: silicon doped with phosphorus.
n-type
Doping with a group V element (phosphorus, arsenic, antimony, 5 outer electrons) leaves one extra free electron per atom. The carriers are electrons (negative), so it is n-type. The atoms are donor atoms.
Figure 11.5: silicon doped with boron.
p-type
Doping with a group III element (boron, aluminium, gallium, indium, 3 outer electrons) leaves a missing electron, a hole, at each atom. Holes act as positive carriers, so it is p-type. The atoms are acceptor atoms. In p-type material holes are the majority carriers and electrons the minority carriers.

11.3  The p-n junction and the diode

Dope one half of a crystal p-type and the other half n-type: where they meet is a p-n junction. Electrons from the n side diffuse across and holes from the p side diffuse the other way. They recombine and leave a region with no free carriers: the depletion layer. The p side of it gains a negative charge and the n side a positive charge, which makes a voltage that opposes further diffusion: the potential barrier (about 0.7 V for silicon, 0.3 V for germanium).

Figure 11.6: the p-n junction.

Biasing is connecting an external voltage across the junction.

Figures 11.8 and 11.9: reverse bias and forward bias.
  • Forward bias: p side to the positive terminal and n side to the negative terminal. The depletion layer narrows. When the applied voltage is above the barrier (greater than 0.7 V for Si, 0.3 V for Ge) a large current flows.
  • Reverse bias: p side to the negative terminal and n side to the positive terminal. The depletion layer widens and no current flows.
Figures 11.10 and 11.11: the symbol of a junction diode and how it looks.

A device made of one p-n junction is a junction diode. The p terminal is the anode (A), the n terminal is the cathode (K). A current flows only when the anode is connected to the positive terminal. On a real diode the white or silver ring marks the cathode. In Activity 11.1 a 2.5 V bulb with an IN4001 diode lights only when the diode is forward biased.

11.4  Rectification and smoothing

An alternating current (AC) keeps reversing; a direct current (DC) flows one way. Dynamos give AC, but many electronic devices need DC. A diode lets current pass one way only, so it can convert AC into DC: rectification.

Figure 11.12: alternating current.
Figure 11.13: half wave rectification.

Half wave rectification: a step-down transformer lowers 230 V AC to a small voltage. The single diode conducts only in the positive half cycle (X positive), so there is a current through R only half of the time.

The output of half wave rectification.
Figure 11.15: a bridge rectifier of four diodes.

Full wave rectification uses four diodes in a bridge. When X is positive, D1 and D4 conduct; when Y is positive, D2 and D3 conduct. In both half cycles the current flows through the load in the same direction.

Figure 11.16: the output of a bridge rectifier.
🔋 Why a 4.5 V battery for a 2.5 V bulb?
  • The current passes through two diodes, each dropping about 0.7 V: 2 × 0.7 = 1.4 V.
  • Voltage left for the bulb: 4.5 − 1.4 = 3.1 V. A 3 V battery would leave only 1.6 V, not enough to light it.

Smoothing: the rectified output rises and falls between zero and a maximum, but most devices want a steady voltage like a battery gives. A large capacitor (for example 1000 μF or 2000 μF) connected in parallel with the output charges while the voltage rises and discharges when it falls, so the output hardly drops to zero. A bigger capacitance smooths better, and full wave output is smoother than half wave.

Figure 11.18: smoothing a half wave output.
Figure 11.19: smoothing a full wave output.

Protection: a rectifier diode in series with a DC appliance protects it if the battery is connected the wrong way round: the diode is then reverse biased and no current flows.

11.5  LEDs and solar cells

When a p-n junction made of a material such as gallium arsenide (GaAs) is forward biased, it gives out light: a light emitting diode (LED). The longer terminal is the anode; the terminal next to the cut on the base is the cathode. LEDs come in red, yellow, green, blue, infrared and ultraviolet, and each colour needs a minimum forward voltage (red about 1.8 V, green 2.2 V, blue 5 V in the textbook table) with a current of 10 to 20 mA. That is why one 1.5 V dry cell cannot light an LED but two cells (3 V) can.

LED colourMaterialMinimum bias voltage
RedGaAs1.8 V
OrangeGaAsP2 V
YellowAlInGaP1.8 V
GreenGaP2.2 V

LEDs are used as indicators, in large TV screens, torches and street and home lighting. They use very little power and last about 50 000 hours. A brighter LED (more current) has a shorter life.

Solar cells are also p-n junctions built so that light falls on the junction. Sunlight on a silicon p-n junction produces a small EMF. Cells joined in series and parallel make a solar panel (for example 12 V to 15 V with a large current). Solar panels were first made for satellites and are now cheap enough to power homes (connected to the mains), clocks, calculators and solar cars. They use free sunlight, give out no harmful substance and last long.

11.6  Transistors

A transistor has two p-n junctions: three semiconductor regions joined as npn or pnp. The three terminals are the emitter (E) and collector (C) at the two ends and the base (B) in the middle. Carriers flow from the emitter to the collector, and the base controls this flow. The arrow head in the symbol marks the emitter and shows the current direction.

Figure 11.25: symbols of npn and pnp transistors.
  • In an npn transistor the carriers are electrons and the current flows from the collector to the emitter (arrow out). In a pnp transistor the carriers are holes and the current flows from the emitter to the collector (arrow in). Only npn transistors are used in this course.
  • Biasing: the emitter-base junction is forward biased (about 0.7 V) and the base-collector junction reverse biased with a higher voltage. For an npn transistor C goes to the positive side and E to the negative side, and B gets a smaller positive voltage than C.
🔊 Transistor as a current amplifier (Activity 11.3)
  • A current flows in the output (collector) circuit only when a current flows in the input (base) circuit.
  • A small base current IB controls a large collector current IC: the small current is amplified.

The transistor also amplifies signals. In Activity 11.4 a small audio signal is fed to the base through a 0.1 μF capacitor (which passes only the changing signal), the base gets its 0.7 V forward bias through a 22 kΩ resistor, and a louder copy of the sound comes from the 8 Ω speaker in the collector circuit.

🚦 Transistor as a switch (Activity 11.5)
  • When the base-emitter voltage is below 0.7 V there is no collector current: the switch is off.
  • From about 0.7 V the collector current starts, and at about 0.8 V it is at its maximum: the switch is on.
The potential divider used to set the base voltage.

The base voltage can be set with a potential divider: two resistors R1 and R2 in series across 3 V carry the same current I = 3 ÷ (R1 + R2), so VB = R2 × 3 ÷ (R1 + R2). Changing R2 changes VB between 0 and 3 V. For example, with R1 = 10 kΩ, VB = 0.7 V needs R2 = 3043 Ω.

Figure 11.30 (Activity 11.6): a switch that turns a bulb on in the dark.

A light dependent resistor (LDR) has a very low resistance in light (about 1 Ω) and a very high one in the dark (about 100 kΩ). With the LDR as R2 and a variable resistor as R1, darkness raises R2, so VB rises past 0.7 V and the transistor switches the bulb on; light makes the bulb go out.

Activity 11.1: a diode lets current pass one way

Textbook · Activity 11.1, p.55

Show the one-way behaviour of a diode with a bulb.

🧰 You need
  • An IN4001 diode, a 2.5 V torch bulb, two 1.5 V dry cells
  • A switch, a circuit board, wires
📝 Steps
  1. Connect the battery, switch, bulb and diode in series, with the diode forward biased (anode towards the positive terminal). Close the switch and watch the bulb.
  2. Reverse the battery (reverse bias) and close the switch again.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The bulb lights only when the diode is forward biased. A junction diode lets a current pass in one direction only.

Activity 11.2: full wave rectification with a bridge

Textbook · Activity 11.2, p.59

Change an alternating current into a direct current with four diodes.

🧰 You need
  • A bicycle dynamo or AC source, four 1N4001 diodes
  • A centre-zero galvanometer, a 100 Ω rheostat, solder and wires
📝 Steps
  1. Solder the four diodes as a bridge with the anodes and cathodes the right way round.
  2. Connect the rheostat and galvanometer across the bridge output, and the dynamo to X and Y.
  3. Turn the dynamo slowly and watch the galvanometer.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The needle deflects only one way: the alternating current has become a direct current (both half cycles flow the same way through the meter).

Activity 11.3: a transistor amplifies current

Textbook · Activity 11.3, p.70–71

Show that a small base current controls a large collector current.

🧰 You need
  • A 2SD400 transistor, two 2.5 V bulbs L1 and L2
  • Two 3 V batteries, two switches, a 10 kΩ volume controller, a circuit board
📝 Steps
  1. Build the circuit: S1, the 3 V battery, VR and L1 form the input circuit; the second battery, S2 and L2 form the output circuit.
  2. Close S1 and adjust VR until L1 just lights, then open S1.
  3. Open and close S1 and S2 in each combination and note the brightness of L1 and L2.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
There is no output current unless there is an input current. A dim L1 (small I_B) gives a bright L2 (large I_C): the transistor amplifies the current.

Activity 11.4: a transistor amplifies a signal

Textbook · Activity 11.4, p.71

Hear a small audio signal made louder.

🧰 You need
  • A 2SD400 transistor, a 22 kΩ resistor, an 8 Ω speaker, a 0.1 μF capacitor
  • A 3 V battery, an audio frequency generator, a circuit board
📝 Steps
  1. Build the circuit and first connect the AF generator directly to the speaker; adjust it until the sound is just audible.
  2. Now feed the same small signal to points A and B of the amplifier.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The sound from the speaker is much louder: the transistor has amplified the audio signal.

Activity 11.5: a transistor as a switch

Textbook · Activity 11.5, p.73

Find the base-emitter voltage at which the transistor switches on.

🧰 You need
  • A 2SD313 transistor, a multimeter, a 2.5 V bulb
  • A 3 V battery, a 10 kΩ volume controller, a 10 kΩ resistor, a switch
📝 Steps
  1. Build the circuit with the volume controller at minimum resistance and the multimeter (2.5 V DC) between base (+) and emitter.
  2. Close the switch, then slowly increase the resistance of the controller, watching the voltmeter and the bulb.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The bulb starts to light at about 0.7 V and is brightest at about 0.8 V. Below 0.7 V the transistor is an open switch (off); above it, a closed switch (on).

Activity 11.6: a light-sensitive switch (LDR)

Textbook · Activity 11.6, p.74

Build a switch that lights a bulb when it gets dark.

🧰 You need
  • A D400 transistor, an LDR, a 10 kΩ volume controller, a 2.5 V bulb
  • A 3 V battery, a circuit board, wires
📝 Steps
  1. Cover the LDR with a finger and adjust the volume controller until the bulb lights.
  2. Take your finger away and let light fall on the LDR.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
In the dark the LDR resistance is high, V_B rises above 0.7 V and the bulb lights. In light the LDR resistance is low and the bulb goes out.

Worked Examples

Step-by-step solutions to the textbook examples
Textbook (extra knowledge) · Ch.11, p.75
Two resistors R1 = 10 kΩ and R2 form a potential divider across 3 V. For what R2 is the voltage V_B at the junction equal to 0.7 V?

V_B = R2 × 3 ÷ (R1 + R2) = 0.7.

3 R2 = 0.7 (10 000 + R2) = 7000 + 0.7 R2.

2.3 R2 = 7000, so R2 = 7000 ÷ 2.3.

Final answer: R2 ≈ 3043 Ω. When the LDR reaches this resistance in the dark, the transistor begins to switch on.
Practice example · potential divider
R1 = 20 kΩ and R2 = 10 kΩ across 3 V. Find V_B. Is a transistor with its base at this point on or off?

V_B = R2 × 3 ÷ (R1 + R2) = 10 000 × 3 ÷ 30 000.

V_B = 1 V, which is more than 0.7 V.

Final answer: V_B = 1 V, so the transistor is on.
Textbook (extra knowledge) · Ch.11, p.60
A 4.5 V battery lights a 2.5 V bulb through a diode bridge. How much voltage is left for the bulb? Would a 3 V battery do?

The current passes through two diodes, each dropping 0.7 V: 2 × 0.7 = 1.4 V.

4.5 − 1.4 = 3.1 V for the bulb; with 3 V it would be 3 − 1.4 = 1.6 V.

Final answer: 3.1 V is left, enough. A 3 V battery leaves only 1.6 V, not enough to light a 2.5 V bulb.
Textbook exercise · Ch.11 Q4
In the LED bridge of Figure 11.31 each LED needs 1.8 V. The current passes through three LEDs in a row. What voltage must the battery give at least?

Three LEDs are in series in the current path: two bridge LEDs and the load LED.

Minimum voltage = 3 × 1.8 = 5.4 V.

Final answer: at least 5.4 V: a 6 V battery works, a 3 V battery does not.

In-text exercise 11.1Reverse the diode · p.59

1.Plot the current through R in Figure 11.13 when only the terminals of the diode are changed (so that X is connected to the cathode) and everything else stays the same.
The diode now conducts in the negative half cycles (when Y is positive), so the current through R flows only then, in the opposite direction to before, and is zero in the positive half cycles.

In-text exercise 11.2Activity 11.2 explained · p.61

1.Explain the reasons for the galvanometer observations in the two instances of Activity 11.2 and show the variation of the current with time graphically for both.
(a) Dynamo connected directly (no bridge): the current alternates, so the centre-zero needle swings to the left and to the right. (b) With the diode bridge: both half cycles flow the same way through the galvanometer, so the needle deflects only one way (it moves in pulses).

Exercise 11.1Semiconductors, LEDs and circuits · p.77

1(i).Explain briefly how metals and semiconductors conduct electricity.
Metals: the electrons in the outer shells are loosely bound and move freely in the metal; when a potential difference is applied these free electrons carry the current. Semiconductors: thermal energy breaks some covalent bonds, giving free electrons and holes. Electrons (negative) and holes (positive) both act as charge carriers and conduct the current.
1(ii).How does the increase of temperature affect the conduction of electricity?
In a conductor the free electrons move more randomly and obstruct the current: the conductivity decreases. In a semiconductor more bonds break, giving more carriers: the conductivity increases.
2(i).A LED is not lighted by one dry cell, but it is lighted when two dry cells are used. Explain why.
A LED lights only when the forward bias exceeds a minimum voltage (about 1.8 V for a red one). One dry cell gives only 1.5 V, which is below this; two cells in series give 3 V, which is above it.
2(ii).Give examples of three instances where we use LEDs in day to day life.
Any three: indicator lights on appliances, large television screens, torches, home lighting, street lighting.
2(iii).The use of white LEDs instead of filament bulbs is increasing. Give reasons.
LEDs use very little power (6 to 8 W instead of 60 W for the same light) and have a very long life (about 50 000 hours against 1200 hours), so they save energy and money.
3.Activity 11.6 uses a transistor to light a bulb in the dark. Design a circuit for a school model that opens a garage door automatically when the headlights of a car fall on it. The motor is a 3 V motor. Draw the circuit, name the components and show where the motor is connected.
Swap the positions of the LDR and the variable resistor of Figure 11.30 so that light (low LDR resistance) raises the base voltage above 0.7 V. Components: a 3 V battery; an LDR from the positive line to the base B; a 10 kΩ variable resistor VR from B to the negative line (together a potential divider); an npn transistor (D400 or D313); and the 3 V motor M in the collector circuit, between the battery positive and the collector C, with the emitter E to the negative line. Set VR so that V_B is below 0.7 V in ordinary darkness; when the headlights shine on the LDR its resistance drops, V_B rises above 0.7 V, a collector current flows and the motor opens the door.
4(i).Figure 11.31: the six LEDs are 1.8 V diodes, in a bridge with two LEDs (D2, D3) opposed across the load. Which LEDs light up when a 6 V battery is connected with X at its negative and Y at its positive terminal?
The current goes from Y through D5, then through the load LED D2 (it points the same way as the current), then D6, and back to X. So D5, D2 and D6 light up. D1, D4 and D3 are reverse biased.
4(ii).Mark the direction of the current with arrows near the LEDs.
The current path is Y → D5 → D2 (downwards through the load) → D6 → X; the arrows go along the green path in the figure above, in the direction the LED triangles point.
4(iii).What happens if the battery is connected to X and Y the opposite way?
Now the current goes X → D1 → D2 → D4 → Y, so D1, D2 and D4 light up (D5, D6 and D3 are reverse biased). The load LED D2 still lights: the bridge makes the current through the load flow the same way whichever way the battery is connected.
4(iv).What would happen if a 3 V battery were used instead of a 6 V battery? Give reasons.
No LED lights up. The current must pass through three 1.8 V LEDs in series, which needs at least 3 × 1.8 = 5.4 V. A 3 V battery is below this (it could not even drive two LEDs, 3.6 V), so the diodes stay below their turn-on voltage and no current flows.

Chapter Summary & Key Terms

One last read before the exam!

📌 Summary — 8 key points

  1. In metals only electrons carry the current. In semiconductors both electrons and holes carry it, and their numbers are equal in a pure crystal.
  2. Doping with a group V element makes an n-type semiconductor; with a group III element, a p-type semiconductor.
  3. A p-n junction has a depletion layer and a potential barrier: about 0.7 V (Si) and 0.3 V (Ge).
  4. Forward bias (p positive) narrows the depletion layer and a current flows; reverse bias widens it and no current flows.
  5. Diodes rectify AC: half wave with one diode, full wave with a bridge of four; a capacitor smooths the output.
  6. LEDs give light when forward biased; solar cells are p-n junctions that make an EMF from light.
  7. A transistor (npn or pnp) has emitter, base and collector; carriers always flow from emitter to collector.
  8. A transistor works as a current amplifier, a signal amplifier and a switch (on above about 0.7 V base-emitter).
