5.1  Seeing, rays and plane mirrors

We see an object only if light from it reaches our eyes. Luminous objects (a candle flame, a bulb) give out their own light. Non-luminous objects only reflect light that falls on them. Materials can be transparent (glass), translucent (tissue paper: light passes but you cannot see clearly) or opaque (stone).

Figure 5.2: a ray is a line with an arrow head. Rays together form beams.
Reflection of light
The change in direction of light rays that fall on a mirror is reflection. First law: the incident ray, the reflected ray and the normal at the point of incidence lie in the same plane. Second law: the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, i = r.
Figure 5.4: AB incident ray, BC reflected ray, BX normal. i = r.
Figure 5.5: the image in a plane mirror.
🪞 Image in a plane mirror
  • It is virtual: no light actually passes through it, so it cannot be caught on a screen.
  • Image distance = object distance (as far behind the mirror as the object is in front).
  • Same size and upright, but laterally inverted: left and right swap. That is why AMBULANCE is printed reversed on the front of the van.

5.2  Curved mirrors

A mirror with a curved reflecting surface is a curved mirror; if the surface is part of a sphere, a spherical mirror. A concave mirror curves inward (dentists' and shaving mirrors give enlarged images). A convex mirror curves outward (vehicle side mirrors and shop security mirrors show a wide view as small images).

  • Centre of curvature (C): the centre of the sphere the mirror is part of.
  • Pole (P): the centre point of the mirror surface.
  • Principal axis: the line through P and C, perpendicular to the mirror at P.
  • Focal point (F): the point where rays parallel to the axis meet (concave) or appear to come from (convex). It lies halfway between P and C.
  • Focal length (f) = distance PF; radius of curvature r = distance PC = 2 f.
✏️ Three easy rays (concave mirror)
  • A ray along the principal axis comes straight back.
  • A ray parallel to the axis is reflected through F. A ray through F is reflected parallel to the axis.
  • A ray through C is reflected back along its own path (the radius is a normal to the surface).

To find the image of a vertical object on the axis, draw two of these rays from the top of the object; the image of the top is where they meet. The foot of the image stays on the axis.

A convex mirror reflects parallel rays as a divergent beam that seems to come from F behind the mirror. Whatever the distance of the object, the image is upright, virtual and smaller, and you cannot catch it on a screen.

5.3  Refraction of light

A pencil in a glass of water looks bent, and a coin at the bottom looks raised. Light bends when it goes from one medium into another with different optical properties. This bending is refraction. The rays from the coin bend away from the normal as they leave the water, so they seem to come from a point higher up: the apparent depth is less than the real depth.

Figure 5.25: apparent depth.

The cause is the change in the speed of light. Light travels at 3 × 108 m s-1 in a vacuum and slower in other media. A medium where light is slower is denser (optically); where it is faster, rarer. A ray bends only if it meets the boundary at an angle other than 90°.

Figure 5.26 (Activity 5.4): a ray through a glass block.

In the glass block: AB is the incident ray, BC the refracted ray (bent towards the normal on entering glass), CD the emergent ray (bent away on leaving). The angles with the normal are the angle of incidence i, of refraction r and of emergence e.

Rare to dense: bends towards the normal.
Dense to rare: bends away from the normal.
Laws of refraction
First law: the incident ray, the refracted ray and the normal lie in the same plane. Second law (Snell's law): sin i ÷ sin r is a constant for two given media. This constant is the refractive index n of the second medium relative to the first.
Refractive index
n = sin i ÷ sin r
no unit. Air to glass ≈ 1.5; air to water ≈ 1.33

If the first medium is a vacuum, n depends on the second medium only; since light in air is almost as fast as in a vacuum, the n of a medium relative to air is used as the n of that medium.

5.4  Total internal reflection

When light goes from a dense to a rare medium it bends away from the normal. Increase the angle of incidence inside the dense medium: at one angle, the critical angle c, the refracted ray runs along the boundary (angle of refraction 90°). For any larger angle all the light is reflected back into the dense medium: total internal reflection.

Figures 5.27 to 5.29: refraction, critical angle and total internal reflection.
MaterialCritical angle
Water49°
Glass42° (the text uses 43°)
Diamond24°
  • Optical fibres: thin flexible glass or plastic fibres. Light entering one end travels along by total internal reflection and leaves at the far end with almost no loss, even over many kilometres. Used in the endoscope (to see inside the body), telephone and Internet links, and decorations.
  • Prisms: a prism with angles 90°, 45°, 45° turns a ray through 90°. The ray enters the first face straight, hits the second face at 45° (more than the critical angle of glass) and is totally reflected, leaving the third face straight. Used in cameras, telescopes and binoculars.
An optical fibre.

5.5  Lenses

A lens is a curved piece of transparent material (glass, plastic, even water) that bends light by refraction. Lenses are in spectacles, cameras, binoculars, telescopes, microscopes, magnifying glasses, and in our eyes. Types: bi-convex and plano-convex (convex lenses), bi-concave and plano-concave (concave lenses).

  • Principal axis: the line through the two centres of curvature. A ray along it passes through undeviated.
  • Optical centre (C): the middle of the lens. A ray through it passes straight, undeviated.
  • Focal point (F): a convex lens bends parallel rays so that they meet at F; it has one F on each side at the same distance, the focal length f. A concave lens spreads parallel rays so that they seem to come from F.
✏️ Three easy rays (convex lens)
  • A ray through the optical centre goes straight on.
  • A ray parallel to the axis passes through F after the lens.
  • A ray through F comes out parallel to the axis.

Figure 5.45: a concave lens.

A concave lens never forms a real image. You see an upright, diminished, virtual image whatever the object distance. (The printed chapter summary says "convex" in two places, but its own sections 5.4.1 and 5.4.2 show it is the concave lens that always gives this.)

  • Hand lens (simple microscope): a convex lens with a handle. With the object closer than f, it gives a magnified, upright, virtual image: for looking at insects and flower parts.
  • Principle of reversibility of light: if a ray is reversed it follows exactly the same path backwards, even after many reflections and refractions.
  • Camera: a convex lens makes a real, inverted image on the film; moving the lens changes the lens-to-film distance so that objects at different distances are sharp. The compound microscope has an objective and an eyepiece lens for a very high magnification.

Hands-on Activities

The textbook activities: try them at home or in class

Activity 5.1: focal length of a concave mirror

Textbook · Activity 5.1, p.112

Find the focal length of a concave mirror using a faraway scene.

🧰 You need
  • A concave mirror, a white screen or paper
  • An open window
📝 Steps
  1. Open the window and hold the concave mirror facing it.
  2. Hold a screen in front of the mirror and move it until a clear image of the outside scene appears.
  3. Measure the distance from the mirror to the screen.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The image is real and upside down. Light from far away objects is almost parallel, so the image is formed at the focal point: the distance you measured is about the focal length.

Activity 5.2: images in a concave mirror

Textbook · Activity 5.2, p.113

Study how the image changes as a candle is moved.

🧰 You need
  • A concave mirror on a stand, a white screen
  • A lit candle
📝 Steps
  1. Find the approximate focal length as in Activity 5.1.
  2. Place the candle about 5 f from the mirror and find the sharp image on the screen.
  3. Move the candle closer in steps and find the image each time. Try very close to the mirror.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The size, position and kind of image change with the object position. When the candle is closer than f, no image appears on the screen: it is virtual and seen by looking into the mirror.

Activity 5.3: convex mirror images

Textbook · Activity 5.3, p.118

Repeat Activity 5.2 with a convex mirror.

🧰 You need
  • A convex mirror, a screen, a candle
📝 Steps
  1. Place the candle in front of the convex mirror and try to catch an image on the screen.
  2. Look into the mirror at different distances.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
No real image can be caught. Looking into the mirror you always see an upright, smaller, virtual image.

Activity 5.4: a ray through a glass block

Textbook · Activity 5.4, p.121–122

Trace the path of a ray into and out of a glass block with pins.

🧰 You need
  • A glass block, white paper, a drawing board
  • Four pins (A, B, C, D), a pencil, a ruler
📝 Steps
  1. Draw round the glass block PQRS on the paper. Stick pin A a little way from face PQ and pin B touching it.
  2. Looking through face SR, put pin C touching SR and pin D further away, so all four pins appear in a straight line.
  3. Remove the block and pins, draw AB, BC, CD and the normals, and mark i, r and e.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The ray bends towards the normal entering glass (rare to dense) and away from the normal leaving it (dense to rare).

Activity 5.5: focusing sunlight with a convex lens

Textbook · Activity 5.5, p.128

See that a convex lens brings parallel light to a point.

🧰 You need
  • A convex lens, a white paper, bright sunlight
📝 Steps
  1. Hold the lens towards the Sun and a paper behind it.
  2. Change the distance until you get a very small bright patch.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
Light from the Sun is parallel. The lens focuses it to one point, the focal point; the lens-to-paper distance is the focal length.

Activity 5.6: images from a convex lens

Textbook · Activity 5.6, p.129

Get a real image of a window and measure the focal length.

🧰 You need
  • A convex lens, a screen, a window
📝 Steps
  1. Hold the lens towards the window and the screen on the other side.
  2. Move the screen until the image is clear and measure the lens-to-screen distance.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
A clear, inverted, real image forms on the screen. The distance measured is the focal length.

Activity 5.7: a concave lens

Textbook · Activity 5.7, p.133

Try to get a real image with a concave lens.

🧰 You need
  • A concave lens, a lit candle, a screen
📝 Steps
  1. Put the candle in front of the lens and try to catch an image on a screen behind it.
  2. Look through the lens at the candle.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
No real image forms. Through the lens you see an upright, smaller, virtual image at any distance.

Worked Examples

Step-by-step solutions to the textbook examples
Practice example · from f = r ÷ 2
The radius of curvature of a concave mirror is 30 cm. Where is its focal point?

The focal point is halfway between the pole and the centre of curvature: r = 2 f.

f = 30 ÷ 2 = 15 cm.

Final answer: the focal point is 15 cm from the pole.
Practice example · from Snell's law
A ray enters glass from air at an angle of incidence whose sine is 0.6. The refractive index of glass is 1.5. Find sin r.

n = sin i ÷ sin r, so sin r = sin i ÷ n.

sin r = 0.6 ÷ 1.5 = 0.4.

Final answer: sin r = 0.4 (r ≈ 24°). The ray bends towards the normal.
Practice example · critical angle of water
The refractive index of water is 1.33. Show that the critical angle is about 49°.

Going air to water, n = sin i ÷ sin r. Reverse the ray (reversibility of light): in water the angle of incidence is c and the angle in air is 90°.

So 1.33 = sin 90° ÷ sin c = 1 ÷ sin c, and sin c = 1 ÷ 1.33 = 0.752.

c = 48.8°.

Final answer: c ≈ 49°, matching the textbook table.
Textbook exercise · Ch.5 Q13
An object 15 mm high is 20 mm from the centre of a convex lens of focal length 30 mm. Find the position, two properties and the height ratio of the image.

The object is closer than f (20 < 30), so the image is virtual, upright and larger, on the same side as the object.

A scale drawing (the parallel ray goes through F, the centre ray goes straight; extend them backwards) puts the image 60 mm from the lens. Check by the lens formula 1/v = 1/u − 1/f: 1/20 − 1/30 = 1/60.

Ratio = image height ÷ object height = 60 ÷ 20 = 3, so the image is 45 mm high.

Final answer: virtual image 60 mm from the lens (same side as the object), upright and larger; image ÷ object = 3.

Exercise 5.2Mirrors, lenses, refraction · p.136–140

1(i).Name two types of mirrors that always produce virtual images.
The plane mirror and the convex mirror.
1(ii)(a).Object between the mirror and the focal point of a concave mirror: is the image upright or inverted?
Upright.
1(ii)(b).Is the image larger or smaller than the object?
Larger than the object.
1(ii)(c).Is the image real or virtual?
Virtual (it cannot be caught on a screen).
1(ii)(d).When the object is moved towards the pole, does the image become smaller or larger?
The image becomes smaller (and moves closer to the mirror); at the pole it is the size of the object. The image is largest when the object is nearest the focal point.
1(iii).At what object distance do you get the largest image from a concave mirror? Is it upright or inverted?
When the object is at the focal point (distance = f) the image is formed at infinity and is very large, and it is inverted. (With the object just inside f the very large image is upright and virtual.)
1(iv).Place an object in front of a convex lens at different positions and observe the image each time. Write two properties common to all those images.
For every position of the object beyond f the image is real (caught on a screen) and inverted. Only when the object is closer than f is the image virtual and upright.
2(i).What is meant by refraction of light?
The bending of light rays when they pass from one medium into another medium (of different optical density).
2(ii).Draw ray diagrams to show refraction when light enters (a) a dense medium from a rare medium, (b) a rare medium from a dense medium.
(a) rare to dense: the ray bends towards the normal. (b) dense to rare: the ray bends away from the normal.
2(iii).Name the rays and angles in the diagram (AB, BC, CD, ABX, YBC, NCD).
AB: incident ray; BC: refracted ray; CD: emergent ray; ABX: angle of incidence; YBC: angle of refraction; NCD: angle of emergence.
3(i).Copy and complete the three ray diagrams of rays entering a rare medium (air) from a dense medium (water): a small angle, the critical angle and an angle greater than the critical angle.
Small angle: the ray refracts away from the normal into air. At the critical angle: the refracted ray runs along the water surface (90°). Greater than the critical angle: the ray is totally reflected back into the water.
3(ii).What is meant by total internal reflection?
When light travelling in a dense medium meets the boundary with a rare medium at an angle of incidence greater than the critical angle, all of it is reflected back into the dense medium.
3(iii).Give an example of a situation where total internal reflection occurs.
Light travelling along an optical fibre (also: a 45°–45°–90° prism in binoculars and cameras).
4.Draw a ray diagram for an image formed by a convex lens when the object is further than twice the focal length. (a) real or virtual? (b) A simple activity to find out? (c) smaller or larger?
(a) Real (and inverted). (b) Hold a white screen behind the lens and move it: if a sharp image appears on the screen, the image is real. (c) Smaller than the object (formed between f and 2f).
5(i)(a).A light ray enters an optical fibre. Draw what happens to the ray inside the fibre.
It hits the wall of the fibre at an angle larger than the critical angle and is totally internally reflected again and again until it leaves the far end.
5(i)(b).State what changes occur to the speed of the ray along its path from the source. (No calculation.)
In air the speed is highest. On entering the glass fibre (a denser medium) the speed decreases. It stays at that lower value inside the fibre, and increases again when the light leaves the fibre into air.
5(ii)(a).At what object distance do you get the largest real image from a convex lens?
When the object is between f and 2f (just beyond f): the nearer to f, the larger the real image.
5(ii)(b).Write two other properties of that image.
It is inverted and formed on the other side of the lens, beyond 2f.
5(ii)(c).At what object distance do you get the smallest real image?
When the object is very far away (at infinity): the image is formed at the focal point and is much smaller than the object.
6.A bag has lenses of focal lengths 10 cm, 20 cm and 25 cm, not marked. Describe a simple activity to identify them.
Hold each lens facing the Sun (or a window) and move a white paper behind it until you get a small bright spot (or a sharp image of the window). Measure the lens-to-paper distance: this is the focal length. The lens that gives about 10 cm, the one giving about 20 cm and the one giving about 25 cm are identified.
7(a)(i).Light changes direction when it passes from air to water. Name the process.
Refraction.
7(a)(ii).Explain why this change of direction occurs.
Light travels at different speeds in air and in water (slower in water). When a ray meets the boundary at an angle, the change in speed makes it bend.
7(b)(i).The diagram shows fish A and B under water and a butterfly above. Draw the path of a ray from the butterfly to the eye of fish B.
The ray from the butterfly travels in a straight line through the air to the water surface, then bends towards the normal as it enters the water, and travels on to the eye of fish B.
7(b)(ii).Explain what the critical angle is.
The angle of incidence in the denser medium (water) for which the angle of refraction in the rare medium (air) is 90°, so the refracted ray runs along the boundary.
7(b)(iii).Explain how rays from fish A could reach the eye of fish B by two different paths. Draw them.
Path 1: directly through the water in a straight line from A to B. Path 2: a ray from A goes up to the water surface at an angle larger than the critical angle, is totally internally reflected back down, and reaches B.
8(a).A ray passes through a glass block into air at X. Complete the diagram, show the angle of incidence i and the angle of refraction r and measure them.
The ray leaves the glass at X and bends away from the normal. Draw the normal at X; i is the angle between the ray in glass and the normal, r the (larger) angle between the ray in air and the normal. Your measured values depend on the drawing, but r is always larger than i (for glass to air, sin r ÷ sin i = 1.5).
8(b).The student increases the angle of incidence and notices that above a certain angle the light no longer passes into air. Explain.
The angle of incidence has become greater than the critical angle of glass, so the light undergoes total internal reflection at the surface and stays in the glass.
9(a).A light signal travels through a glass optical fibre. State two changes to the light when it enters the fibre from air at B.
(1) Its speed decreases. (2) Its direction changes: it bends towards the normal (refraction).
9(b).Explain why the light, after hitting point P, travels along the path shown.
At P the angle of incidence inside the glass is greater than the critical angle, so the light is totally internally reflected and stays inside the fibre.
10(a).Complete the table: nature, size and position of the images in the eye, a projector and a magnifying glass.
Eye: real, inverted, smaller (diminished), formed on the retina behind the lens. Projector: real, inverted, magnified, formed on the screen far beyond 2f on the other side. Magnifying glass: virtual, upright, magnified, further from the lens than the object (same side as the object).
10(b).An object is closer than f to a convex lens. (i) Complete the ray diagram and draw the image. (ii) Describe three properties of the image.
The parallel ray goes through F, the centre ray goes straight; traced backwards they meet at the image. It is virtual, upright and magnified (and on the same side as the object).
11.An object OB is in front of a convex lens (focal points F and F′). (a) Draw two rays from B. (b) Draw the image I. (c) Find the ratio of image size to object size.
The object is between f and 2f, so the image is real, inverted, larger and beyond 2f. Draw the ray parallel to the axis (through F after the lens) and the ray through the lens centre; they meet at the top of the image I. Measure the heights from your grid: ratio = image height ÷ object height. For the grid in the book (object about 1.75 f from the lens) this is about 1.3 (4/3).
12.A convex lens forms a real image. State two changes to the image when the object is moved towards F.
The image becomes larger and moves further from the lens (it forms further away on the other side).
13(a).A 15 mm high object is at O, 20 mm from the centre of a convex lens of focal length 30 mm. By a scale drawing find the position of the image.
Draw the parallel ray (through F) and the centre ray and extend them backwards: the image is on the same side as the object, 60 mm from the lens (twice as far as the object). Check: 1/v = 1/20 − 1/30 = 1/60.
13(b).State two properties of the image.
Virtual and upright (it is also magnified).
13(c).By measuring the heights of the image and object, find the ratio of their heights.
Image height = 45 mm, object height = 15 mm, so the ratio is 45 ÷ 15 = 3.

Chapter Summary & Key Terms

One last read before the exam!

📌 Summary — 9 key points

  1. There are plane mirrors and curved mirrors (convex and concave).
  2. Images in plane mirrors are virtual, upright and the same size as the object.
  3. The incident ray, reflected ray and normal lie in one plane, and i = r.
  4. A convex mirror always forms an upright, smaller, virtual image.
  5. The bending of light going from one medium to another is refraction. Rare to dense: towards the normal; dense to rare: away from it.
  6. The incident ray, refracted ray and normal lie in one plane, and n = sin i ÷ sin r.
  7. At the critical angle the refracted ray travels along the boundary; beyond it the ray is totally internally reflected.
  8. Light travels along optical fibres by total internal reflection.
  9. Lenses are bi-convex, plano-convex, bi-concave or plano-concave. A concave lens always gives an upright, diminished, virtual image; a convex lens gives real or virtual images depending on the object distance.
