Turning Effect of Forces

Push a door near the handle and it swings open easily; push near the hinge and it's really hard — why? Why does a longer spanner make loosening a nut so much easier? The answer is the turning effect (moment) of a force! We'll also see exactly how to balance a see-saw. Deep explanations below.

11.1a  A Force Can Turn Things Too!

So far we've seen force push, pull, and lift an object. But force has another job — it can also turn (rotate) an object! A door is a great example — push near the hinge and it's really hard to open, but push near the handle and it opens easily.

This turning tendency is called the moment of a force. It's what decides whether an object turns/rotates about a particular point or axis. Notice what point the door turns about? Its hinge — that point is called the turning axis (pivot).

11.1b  How Do You Calculate a Moment?

Measure the force needed to open a door at various points (at different distances from the hinge) with a spring balance — the farther from the hinge you push, the less force you need. But here's a surprising fact — multiply that force by the distance, and the answer is almost always the same value!

Moment of a Force
Moment = Force (N) × perpendicular distance from the axis to the force's line of action (m)
Unit: N m (newton-metre). "Perpendicular distance" means the shortest distance from the axis, measured at a right angle to the force.

This is exactly why a longer spanner loosens a nut more easily — holding it farther from the nut (the turning axis) gives you a bigger moment for the same effort.

11.1c  Equilibrium — How a See-Saw Balances

Hang a rod at its centre, with weights hanging on both sides — when does it stay perfectly balanced? One side going up or down happens because of a moment — so for balance, the moments on both sides must be exactly equal.

Principle of Moments
For an object to be in equilibrium, the sum of the moments on one side of the pivot must equal the sum of the moments on the other side.

Split them into "clockwise" moments on one side and "anticlockwise" moments on the other: for balance, total clockwise moment = total anticlockwise moment. This is exactly why, on a real see-saw, the heavier kid has to sit closer to the middle and the lighter kid has to sit at the far end!

11.2  Couple — Turning with a Pair of Forces

When you turn a water tap, how do you do it? You push one finger "up" and another "down" — equal forces, opposite directions, not along the same line. This is called a couple.

What is a Couple?
A couple is a pair of equal, parallel forces acting on an object in opposite directions, causing it to turn or rotate about an axis.

Using a couple makes turning something much easier — because the two forces are opposite, they don't move the object anywhere (no net force at all), they only turn it. That's why turning a steering wheel with two hands, or a screwdriver with your fingers, feels so light.

Moment of a Couple
Moment of couple = Force × perpendicular distance between the forces' lines of action
Also measured in N m — but here you use the distance between the two forces, not the distance from a single axis.
1 / 4
⚠️ Common mistakes
  • "Distance" always means perpendicular distance! If a force acts at an angle, don't use the plain distance — use the distance measured at a right angle (90°) from the axis to the force's line of action.
  • Don't forget clockwise vs anticlockwise. In balance problems, add up moments on one side and set them equal to the other side — don't wrongly lump everything together as if it's all the same direction.
  • A couple has no net force, but it DOES have a net moment. Because the two forces are equal and opposite, they don't push/pull the object anywhere — but they still turn it.
  • The unit of moment is N m — not N! Force alone is in N, but the moment of a force is always in N m (newton-metre).

Activity 1 — Opening a door

Textbook · p.199–200

Find how the place where you push a door changes the force needed to open it.

🧰 You need
  • A door fixed with hinges
  • A newton balance (spring balance)
📝 Steps
  1. Mark four points A, B, C and D at the same horizontal level on the door (Fig. 11.1).
  2. Attach the newton balance at A and pull to open the door. Read the force at the moment the door just starts to move.
  3. Repeat at B, C and D and fill in Table 11.1: the force, the perpendicular distance from the hinge axis, and force × distance (N m).
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The farther from the hinge (the axis of rotation) you apply the force, the smaller the force needed to open the door. Pushing close to the hinges is hard. The turning effect depends on the perpendicular distance of the force from the axis.

Activity 2 — Does the size of the force matter?

Textbook · p.201–203

Find how the magnitude of the force affects the turning effect.

🧰 You need
  • A wooden strip with holes O, A, B, C, D (15 cm, 30 cm, 45 cm, 60 cm from O)
  • Two rubber washers, a drill, a screw, a newton balance
  • A table or board
📝 Steps
  1. Fix the strip to the board at O with the screw between the two washers (Fig. 11.3).
  2. Hook the newton balance at D and pull at right angles to the strip, until it just starts to turn. Read the force.
  3. Tighten the screw half a turn and find the force again. Then tighten one full turn and find it again.
  4. Record the readings in Table 11.2.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
As the screw is tightened, the force needed to turn the strip increases (the textbook sample readings: 2 N at the start, 5 N after half a turn, 9 N after a full turn). So the turning effect depends on the size of the force.

Activity 3 — Does the distance matter?

Textbook · p.203–204

Test whether the perpendicular distance from the pivot affects the turning effect.

🧰 You need
  • The same strip, screw and washers as Activity 2
  • A newton balance and a thread
📝 Steps
  1. Tie a thread through the strip near A and attach the newton balance. With the screw as set at the start, pull until the strip just turns. Note the force F₁ and the distance from O.
  2. Tighten the screw a quarter turn more. Keep the reading at F₁ and move the balance away from O until the strip just turns again. Measure the distance.
  3. Tighten another quarter turn (half a turn in total) and repeat. Fill in Table 11.3.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
With the same force F₁ (1.5 N in the textbook sample), the balance has to be placed farther and farther from O as the screw gets tighter (sample distances 15, 32 and 55). So the turning effect depends on the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the force.

Worked Textbook Examples

Tap through each step — mark values shown as in an examiner's scheme
Worked Example · Ch.11, p.205
A uniform 1 m rod AB is suspended and balanced at its centre. A 4 N weight hangs at end B (0.5 m from the pivot). (i) Find the moment caused by the 4 N force. (ii) What weight, hung at point C (0.4 m from the pivot), would keep the rod balanced?

(i) Moment = Force × distance = 4 N × 0.5 m

= 2 N m

(ii) For balance: left moment = right moment, i.e. w × 0.4 = 4 × 0.5

Make w the subject: w = (4 × 0.5) ÷ 0.4

= 2 ÷ 0.4 = 5 N
Where marks are lost: not setting up the balance condition (w × distance = 4 × distance) correctly.
Final answers: (i) Moment = 2 N m   (ii) Required weight w = 5 N.
Worked Example · Ch.11, p.208 (Couple)
A thin plate pivoted at O has a 10 N force upward and a 10 N force downward acting on it — one at 0.4 m from O, the other at 0.3 m from O (on opposite sides). Find the moment of this couple.

Total perpendicular distance between the two forces = 0.4 + 0.3

= 0.7 m

Moment of couple = Force × distance = 10 N × 0.7 m

= 7 N m
Where marks are lost: using only 0.4 or only 0.3 — you need to add both distances to get the total.
Final answer: Moment of the couple = 7 N m.

Practice Exercises

Every practice question from the textbook — each with a full worked solution

Exercise 11.1Moments

1.A rod XY is 0.8 m long. A 2 N weight hangs at end X. For the rod to remain balanced, at what distance from the pivot should a 4 N weight be hung on the other side?
X is 0.4 m from the centre (half of 0.8 m). Balance: 2 × 0.4 = 4 × x
x = 0.8 ÷ 4 = 0.2 m
2.Three everyday situations using the turning effect of a force: (1) using a spanner to loosen a bolt (2) applying force to a bicycle pedal (3) using a wheelbarrow. Describe how the moment acts in each.
(1) Spanner: applying force at the far end of the handle gives a large moment (since the distance from the bolt — the pivot — is large), so the bolt loosens with less effort.
(2) Bicycle pedal: the force applied by the foot on the pedal creates a moment about the crank's axle, turning the chain wheel.
(3) Wheelbarrow: the force applied at the handles creates a large moment about the wheel's axle (the pivot), lifting the load with less effort.

Exercise 11.2Couples

1(i).Give two examples of situations where a couple acts.
(1) Turning a vehicle's steering wheel with two hands in opposite directions. (2) Turning a water tap with two fingers in opposite directions (or turning a screwdriver).
1(ii).A thin plate pivoted at O has forces applied as shown (10 N forces, at 0.4 m and 0.3 m from O, on opposite sides). Find the moment of the couple.
10 N × (0.4 + 0.3) m = 7 N m (see Worked Example 2 above)

Chapter ExerciseEverything together

1.A 1.2 m rod AB is suspended and balanced at its centre. A 10 N weight hangs at end A (0.6 m from the pivot). Find the force that must be applied at 0.3 m from the pivot, on the other side, to keep the rod balanced.
Balance: 10 × 0.6 = F × 0.3
F = 6 ÷ 0.3 = 20 N
2.An 8 m beam AC has 10 N down at 2 m from A, 20 N up at the centre (B, 4 m), and 10 N down at 6 m. Find the moment caused by these three forces about (a) point A (b) point B (c) point C.
(a) About A: anticlockwise (20N × 4m = 80) vs clockwise (10N × 2m + 10N × 6m = 20+60 = 80) → net moment = 0 (balanced)
(b) About B: anticlockwise (10N × 2m = 20) vs clockwise (10N × 2m = 20) → net moment = 0 (the 20N force passes right through B, so it has zero moment there)
(c) About C: clockwise (20N × 4m = 80) vs anticlockwise (10N × 6m + 10N × 2m = 60+20 = 80) → net moment = 0 (balanced)
எல்லா மூன்று புள்ளிகள் பற்றியும் நிகர திருப்பம் = 0 N m / Net moment about all three points = 0 N m
This shows a beautiful fact — if a system is in equilibrium, the net moment about ANY point is zero, not just about the pivot!

Chapter Summary & Key Terms

One last read before the exam!

📌 Summary — 4 key points

  1. The turning (or rotating) effect produced about a point or an axis when a force acts on an object is called the moment of the force (turning effect).
  2. Moment of a force = force × perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force. Its unit is N m.
  3. Applying a couple is the easiest way to turn (or rotate) an object in a given direction.
  4. A couple is a pair of equal, parallel forces acting in opposite directions on the object.
