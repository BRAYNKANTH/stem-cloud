19.1  Static electricity and current electricity

Electricity is a very important form of energy. Bulbs, irons and fans all work with it. It has two forms: static electricity, charges that sit on the surface of insulators and do not flow, and current electricity, where charges flow.

🧪 Try it: static charges
  • Rub a drinking straw with a piece of cotton cloth and bring it near tiny bits of paper: the bits are attracted. A straw that was not rubbed attracts nothing.
  • A plastic rod, pen or comb rubbed on your hair attracts tiny bits of paper or rigifoam too.
Figure 19.3: the particles of an atom.

All materials are made of atoms. An atom has protons (positive), neutrons (no charge) and electrons (negative). Protons and neutrons are in the centre, the nucleus; electrons move around it. Only electrons can be removed easily.

  • If rubbing removes electrons from the surface, the surface becomes positively (+) charged.
  • If the surface receives electrons from the cloth, it becomes negatively (−) charged.
  • Charges that stay still on an object are electrostatic charges. When they start to move, they make an electric current.

In Activity 19.1 a PVC rod is charged by rubbing it with polythene and touched to a neon bulb whose other terminal is earthed. The stored charges flow out through the bulb and it lights up for a moment. Charges flowing like this are an electric current.

Electric current
A current of electric charges flowing through a conductor is an electric current.

19.2  Electricity flowing through conductors

Materials that let a current of electrons pass easily are conductors. All metals (copper, aluminium, iron) are conductors. The electrons in the outermost shell of a metal atom come loose easily and move at random between the atoms. These are the free electrons, and they are why electricity flows easily through metals.

Figures 19.5 and 19.6: free electrons in a metal.

The negative terminal of a cell repels electrons; the positive terminal attracts them. Join the two terminals with a conductor and the free electrons all start to drift from the negative terminal to the positive terminal.

Figures 19.8 and 19.9: the conventional current goes from + to −; the electrons go from − to +.
➡️ Two directions to remember
  • The electrons really flow from the negative to the positive terminal.
  • By convention the electric current is said to flow the opposite way: from the positive to the negative terminal.

The SI unit of current is the ampere (A), measured with an ammeter. The ammeter must be connected so that the whole current passes through it, that is, in series.

Figure 19.11: connecting an ammeter to a circuit.

19.3  Potential difference and electromotive force

Water runs faster from a tank placed higher, because the pressure difference between the tank and the tap is bigger. The current in a circuit is like this water flow: the source of electricity acts like the tank.

The water-tank picture of a circuit.
Potential difference and EMF
The electric "pressure difference" is the potential difference, measured in volts (V). The force with which the negative terminal of the source pushes electrons into the outside circuit is the electromotive force (EMF).

The EMF of a cell equals the potential difference across its terminals when no current is drawn. When a current is drawn it also passes through the cell, which has its own resistance, so some voltage is lost inside; subtract it from the EMF to get the potential difference given to the outside circuit.

Because it is measured in volts, the potential difference is also called the voltage. It is measured with a voltmeter, whose two terminals are connected across the two points, that is, in parallel.

Figure 19.13: connecting a voltmeter across a bulb.
🔬 Activity 19.2 in one picture
  • Connection 1: both positive terminals go to the bulb: no potential difference, no current, bulb off.
  • Connection 2: both negative terminals go to the bulb: again none, bulb off.
  • Connection 3: one positive and one negative terminal: there is a potential difference, a current flows and the bulb lights.

So for a current to flow through a conductor, a potential difference must exist across it.

19.4  Ohm's law

When a potential difference is put across a conductor, a current flows. In Activity 19.3 a nichrome coil is connected to two dry cells, a rheostat, an ammeter and a voltmeter. The rheostat changes the current, and you record five pairs of readings. The switch is closed only briefly, so that the coil does not heat up and the temperature stays constant.

Figure 19.18: V against I is a straight line through the origin.

For each reading divide V by I. If the temperature was kept constant, the ratio V/I is always the same number. This was discovered by the German scientist Georg Simon Ohm.

Ohm's law
When the temperature of a conductor stays constant, the current I through it is directly proportional to the potential difference V across it: I ∝ V, so V ÷ I = constant. This constant is the resistance R of the conductor.
Ohm's law
V = I R    R = V ÷ I    I = V ÷ R
V in volts (V), I in amperes (A), R in ohms (Ω)

The unit of resistance is the ohm (Ω): a conductor has a resistance of 1 Ω if a current of 1 A flows when 1 V is across it. Resistance is measured with an ohm meter.

Example 1
A current of 1.5 A flows through a bulb of resistance 6 Ω. Find the potential difference across the bulb.
V = I R = 1.5 × 6 = 9 V

19.5  What changes the resistance of a conductor?

The resistance of a piece of conductor depends on three things: its cross-sectional area, its length and the material it is made of. In Activity 19.4 the same cells push current through seven wires, and the ammeter readings differ because the resistances differ.

📏 The three rules
  • Thicker wire (bigger cross-sectional area): resistance decreases.
  • Longer wire: resistance increases.
  • Same length and area but a different metal: different currents for the same voltage, because of a property of the material called resistivity.

19.6  Resistors

A tap controls the flow of water in a pipe by putting an obstacle in the way. In a circuit, raising the resistance lowers the current. Components made to give a chosen resistance are called resistors. In Activity 19.5 you connect a 5 Ω, 10 Ω or 20 Ω resistor in the torch bulb circuit: the larger the resistor, the dimmer the bulb, so the current falls as the resistance rises.

  • Fixed resistors: a thin film of carbon on an insulator, or a coil of a high-resistance material such as nichrome. Their value cannot be changed (for example 10 Ω, 100 Ω, 1.2 kΩ).
  • Variable resistors: the value can be changed by hand or with a screw: pre-adjustment resistors, rheostats, volume controls. Used in radio volume controls, to adjust circuits and to vary currents in experiments.
  • Light dependent resistors (LDR): made of chemicals such as cadmium sulphide. In the dark the resistance is high; in light it is low. Used in circuits that must react to the amount of light.
Figure 19.25: the colour bands of a resistor.

The value of a fixed resistor is often shown by colour bands. With four bands, the first two give the digits, the third gives the power of ten to multiply by, and the fourth (set a little apart) gives the tolerance. Gold and silver as the third band mean 10-1 and 10-2.

Example
A resistor has bands brown, black, red, silver. (i) Its value? (ii) Its tolerance? (iii) The range its true value can have?
(i) brown = 1, black = 0, red = 2: value = 10 × 102 = 1000 Ω
(ii) silver: tolerance = ± 10 %
(iii) variation = 1000 × 10 ÷ 100 = 100 Ω. Range = (1000 − 100) to (1000 + 100) = 900 Ω to 1100 Ω

19.7  Combining resistors: series and parallel

If you cannot find a resistor of the value you need, you can join several. There are two basic ways: series and parallel.

Figure 19.29: resistors in series. The same current I flows through each.

In series the same current I flows through every resistor. The potential differences across them are V₁ = I R₁, V₂ = I R₂, V₃ = I R₃ and they add up to the supply voltage: V = I R = I R₁ + I R₂ + I R₃.

Equivalent resistance, series
R = R₁ + R₂ + R₃
R is the single resistance that could replace all the resistors
Example (series)
A 10 Ω and a 2 Ω resistor in series are connected to a 6 V supply. Find the equivalent resistance and the current.
R = 10 + 2 = 12 Ω. I = V ÷ R = 6 ÷ 12 = 0.5 A
Figure 19.30: resistors in parallel. The total current divides among them.

In parallel the total current divides among the resistors: I = I₁ + I₂ + I₃. Each resistor has the same potential difference V across it, so V/R = V/R₁ + V/R₂ + V/R₃, which gives the rule below.

Equivalent resistance, parallel
1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃
The reciprocal of the equivalent resistance is the sum of the reciprocals
Example (parallel)
A 12 Ω and a 6 Ω resistor are connected in parallel to a 6 V supply. Find (i) the equivalent resistance, (ii) the total current, (iii) the current in each resistor.
(i) 1/R = 1/12 + 1/6 = 1/12 + 2/12 = 3/12 = 1/4, so R = 4 Ω
(ii) I = V ÷ R = 6 ÷ 4 = 1.5 A
(iii) 12 Ω: I = 6 ÷ 12 = 0.5 A. 6 Ω: I = 6 ÷ 6 = 1 A. (0.5 + 1 = 1.5 A)

19.8  Extra: electric shock and safety

An electric shock happens when your body becomes part of a path along which electrons can flow. A complete circuit and a potential difference are both needed. With only one contact point, or two points at the same voltage, there is no danger.

Figure 19.32: a bird on a high-voltage line gets no shock.

People are different from birds: they usually stand on the ground. One side of a power system is deliberately connected to earth, so a person touching a single live wire connects two points of the circuit: the wire and the earth.

"It is current that kills" is largely true, but a current needs a voltage to push it. The current through the body is I = V ÷ R: the higher the voltage, the larger the current, and the larger the body's resistance, the smaller the current. Body resistance is not fixed: it changes from person to person and with how the skin touches (hand to hand, hand to foot and so on). That is why HIGH VOLTAGE signs are put up.

Hands-on Activities

The textbook activities: try them at home or in class

Demonstration: static charge on a straw and a comb

Textbook · Figures 19.1–19.2, p.140

See that rubbing gives an object the power to attract light things.

🧰 You need
  • A drinking straw, a plastic rod, pen or comb
  • A piece of cotton cloth, your hair
  • Tiny bits of paper or rigifoam
📝 Steps
  1. Rub the straw well with the cotton cloth and bring it close to the bits of paper.
  2. Bring an unrubbed straw close to the bits too.
  3. Rub a plastic rod, pen or comb on your hair and try again.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
Only rubbed objects attract the bits. Rubbing moves electrons from one surface to the other, so the surfaces get static electric charges.

Activity 19.1: lighting a neon bulb with static charges

Textbook · Activity 19.1, p.142

Show that moving static charges make an electric current.

🧰 You need
  • A piece of PVC tube, a piece of polythene
  • A neon bulb, conducting wires, a stand
📝 Steps
  1. Connect wires to the neon bulb and connect one terminal of the bulb to the earth.
  2. Charge the PVC rod by rubbing it with polythene.
  3. Touch the free terminal of the bulb with the charged rod. Repeat several times and watch the bulb.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The neon bulb flashes each time. The stored charges flow out through the wires and the bulb; charges flowing like this are an electric current.

Activity 19.2: a potential difference is needed

Textbook · Activity 19.2, p.148–149

Find out what must exist across a bulb for a current to flow.

🧰 You need
  • Two dry cells, a bulb, conducting wires
  • A voltmeter and an ammeter
📝 Steps
  1. Connection 1: join the two positive terminals to the two ends of the bulb. Connection 2: join the two negative terminals. Connection 3: join one positive and one negative terminal.
  2. The voltmeter measures the voltage across the bulb; the ammeter measures the current through it.
  3. Record the current, the potential difference and whether the bulb lights, for each connection.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
Only connection 3 gives a potential difference across the bulb, so only there a current flows and the bulb lights. A current flows through a conductor only if a potential difference exists across it.

Activity 19.3: current and potential difference (Ohm)

Textbook · Activity 19.3, p.150–151

Find the relationship between the current through a conductor and the voltage across it.

🧰 You need
  • A nichrome wire coil, a rheostat, a switch
  • Two dry cells, a voltmeter, an ammeter, connecting wires
📝 Steps
  1. Connect the circuit: voltmeter across the coil, ammeter in series.
  2. Close the switch, quickly read the meters and switch off (so the coil does not heat up).
  3. Change the rheostat and repeat for at least five sets. Work out V ÷ I each time.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
V ÷ I is the same every time (as long as the temperature is constant). This constant is the resistance, and the law is V = I R (Ohm's law).

Activity 19.4: what changes the resistance?

Textbook · Activity 19.4, p.153–154

Compare wires of different area, material and length.

🧰 You need
  • Three 1 m nichrome wires of different thickness, a thin copper wire, thin iron wires of two lengths
  • Two dry cells, an ammeter, a switch, a wooden board
📝 Steps
  1. Connect the circuit and touch terminal X to the end of each wire in turn.
  2. Record the ammeter reading for each of the seven wires.
  3. Compare wires 1, 2, 3 (area); wires 3, 4, 5 (material); wires 5, 6, 7 (length).
💡 What should you find? (tap)
A thicker wire gives a larger current (smaller resistance); a longer wire gives a smaller current (larger resistance); different metals give different currents. So resistance depends on area, length and material.

Activity 19.5: how a resistor controls the current

Textbook · Activity 19.5, p.155

See what a larger resistance does to the brightness of a bulb.

🧰 You need
  • A small torch bulb, a switch, an ammeter, two dry cells
  • Resistors of 5 Ω, 10 Ω and 20 Ω
📝 Steps
  1. Connect the circuit with a gap between A and B.
  2. Connect the 5 Ω resistor between A and B and watch the bulb.
  3. Replace it with 10 Ω and then 20 Ω, noting the brightness each time.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The bulb gets dimmer as the resistor value rises. The current through a circuit decreases when the resistance increases.

Worked Examples

Step-by-step solutions to the textbook examples
Textbook example · Ch.19, p.152
A current of 1.5 A flows through a bulb which has a resistance of 6 Ω. Find the potential difference across the bulb.

Use Ohm's law, V = I R.

V = 1.5 × 6.

Final answer: 9 V.
Textbook example · Ch.19, p.158–159
A resistor has colour bands brown, black, red and silver. (i) Find its resistance. (ii) What is its tolerance? (iii) What is the range of its true value?

(i) Brown = 1 and black = 0 are the digits (10). Red = 2 is the power of ten, so the value is 10 × 10² = 1000 Ω.

(ii) The silver band means a tolerance of ± 10 %.

(iii) Variation = 1000 × 10 ÷ 100 = 100 Ω, so the range is (1000 − 100) Ω to (1000 + 100) Ω.

Final answer: (i) 1000 Ω, (ii) ± 10 %, (iii) 900 Ω to 1100 Ω.
Textbook example · Ch.19, p.162
A 10 Ω and a 2 Ω resistor are connected in series to a 6 V power supply. (i) Find the equivalent resistance. (ii) Find the current in the circuit.

(i) In series R = R₁ + R₂ = 10 + 2 = 12 Ω.

(ii) Use V = I R: I = V ÷ R = 6 ÷ 12.

Final answer: (i) 12 Ω, (ii) 0.5 A.
Textbook example · Ch.19, p.163–165
A 12 Ω and a 6 Ω resistor are connected in parallel to a 6 V supply. (i) Equivalent resistance? (ii) Current in the circuit? (iii) Current in each resistor?

(i) 1/R = 1/12 + 1/6 = 1/12 + 2/12 = 3/12 = 1/4, so R = 4 Ω.

(ii) I = V ÷ R = 6 ÷ 4 = 1.5 A.

(iii) Each resistor has the full 6 V across it. 12 Ω: I = 6 ÷ 12 = 0.5 A. 6 Ω: I = 6 ÷ 6 = 1 A.

Final answer: (i) 4 Ω, (ii) 1.5 A, (iii) 0.5 A and 1 A. Check: 0.5 + 1 = 1.5 A, the total.

Chapter Summary & Key Terms

One last read before the exam!

📌 Summary — 9 key points

  1. By rubbing certain materials on one another, electrons are exchanged between them.
  2. An electric current is a flow of electric charges.
  3. The direction of the conventional current is from the positive terminal to the negative terminal.
  4. The potential difference between the terminals of a cell when no current passes through it is the electromotive force of the cell.
  5. Ohm's law: at constant temperature the current through a conductor is proportional to the potential difference across it (V = I R).
  6. The property that obstructs the current is the electrical resistance. It depends on the cross-sectional area, the length and the material.
  7. Resistors are joined in two main ways: series and parallel.
  8. Series: R = R₁ + R₂ + R₃. Parallel: 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃.
  9. A single resistance equal to the total resistance of a group of resistors is the equivalent resistance.
