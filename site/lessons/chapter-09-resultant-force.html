Resultant Force

Why doesn't one person's push move a stalled car, but three people's push does? How do we decide who wins a tug-of-war? When several forces act together, we can combine them all into one single resultant force. Let's see exactly how, deeply, with real examples.

9.1  Several Forces At Once — Which One "Wins"?

A car has stalled on the road, and one person pushes — but that one person's force isn't enough to move the heavy car. A second person joins in — still not enough. Finally a third person joins, and the car starts moving! What does this tell us? When more than one force acts on the same object, they all combine to act like a single force — this single, equivalent force is called the resultant force.

What is a Resultant Force?
When more than one force is applied to an object, the single equivalent force that produces the same combined effect as all of them together is called the resultant force.

This idea matters a lot — in real life, an object almost never has just one force on it (gravity, friction from the ground, and any force you apply could all act at once). To predict what the object will do, you first add up all those forces into one resultant force — this resultant is what actually decides the motion (remember Newton's laws — it's the unbalanced force that changes motion!).

9.2  Resultant of Two Forces on the Same Line

Think of fishermen pulling in a fishing net — everyone pulls the same rope in the same direction. This makes it easy to haul in the net together. That's because when everyone's force is in the same direction, the resultant is simply the sum of all their forces.

Two forces in the SAME direction
Resultant = Force 1 + Force 2
e.g. Two kids pull a box in the same direction with 8 N and 6 N — resultant = 8+6 = 14 N

But in a tug-of-war, two teams pull in opposite directions — the forces are in opposite directions. Now the resultant isn't a sum, it's a difference — the difference between the two forces, in the direction of whichever team pulled harder.

Two forces in OPPOSITE directions
Resultant = larger force − smaller force (in the direction of the larger force)
e.g. 5 N one way, 2 N the opposite way — resultant = 5 + (−2) = 3 N (in the direction of the 5 N)
💡 Easy trick
  • Pick one direction as positive (+) and the opposite as negative (−). Then just add all the forces up — the sign (+/−) automatically gives you the correct direction!

9.3  Resultant of Two Parallel Forces

A single person pushes a stalled car with 150 N — it doesn't move, that force isn't enough to overcome the car's friction and weight. A second person helps by pushing in the same direction with 200 N — together they now apply 350 N — and now it moves!

Two parallel forces, same direction
Resultant = Force 1 + Force 2 = 150 N + 200 N = 350 N
"Parallel" just means both forces point the same way — they don't have to act along exactly the same line (e.g. pushing at different points on the car still works).

The textbook demonstrates this directly with a simple experiment — spring balances at three points on a wooden strip attached to a trolley, showing that a third upward-pulling force equals the sum of two downward-pulling forces at the other points — proving that the resultant of parallel forces equals their sum.

9.4  Resultant of Two Non-Parallel (Inclined) Forces

Everything we've seen so far was forces along the same direction or line. But what happens when two forces hit an object at different angles (inclined to each other)? For example, if forces P and Q act on an object at an angle, the object won't move in P's direction, and won't move in Q's direction either — it will move in a new direction somewhere between the two!

Resultant of Non-Parallel Forces
The resultant of two non-parallel forces acts in a direction between the two forces.

You'll study this idea more deeply, with proper angle calculations (the parallelogram of forces), in higher grades. For now, just remember: "when directions differ, the resultant direction lies somewhere between them."

⚠️ Common mistakes
  • Never forget direction! First check if the forces are in the same direction or opposite. If opposite, you must subtract, not add — otherwise the answer is wrong.
  • The resultant always points in the direction of the LARGER force (in the opposite-direction case). Don't accidentally state the direction of the smaller force.
  • "Parallel" doesn't mean "same line"! Parallel forces point the same way, but they can act at different points on the object — the addition rule still applies the same.
  • The resultant's unit is always N (newton) — whether you add or subtract, the unit of force never changes.

Activity 9.1 — Two forces in the same direction

Textbook · p.166

Find the resultant of two forces that act in the same direction along one straight line.

🧰 You need
  • A trolley
  • Three spring balances (newton balances): A, B and C
  • Two pulleys and a ring
📝 Steps
  1. Place the trolley on a table. Tie strong threads to a ring, and pass two of them over the pulleys to spring balances B and C. Attach spring balance A to the trolley (Fig. 9.5).
  2. Pull with spring balances B and C so that the forces act together in the same direction, and note their readings.
  3. Note the reading of spring balance A.
  4. Find the relationship between the three readings. Repeat several times with different pulls.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The reading of A equals the sum of the readings of B and C. So when two forces act in the same direction on a straight line, their resultant is the sum of the two forces.

Activity 9.2 — Two forces in opposite directions

Textbook · p.169

See what happens to a trolley when two forces pull it in opposite directions.

🧰 You need
  • A trolley
  • Two spring balances, A and B
  • Two identical pulleys and spring-balance stands
📝 Steps
  1. Put the trolley on a table. Tie a thread to each side, pass the free ends over the pulleys, and attach spring balances A and B (Fig. 9.7).
  2. Case 1: pull each balance with 4 N and watch the trolley.
  3. Case 2: pull A with 4 N and B with 6 N and watch the trolley.
  4. Case 3: pull A with 6 N and B with 6 N and watch the trolley.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
Cases 1 and 3: the trolley does not move, because equal opposite forces cancel. Case 2: it moves towards B, as if pulled by a single 2 N force (6 − 4). So the resultant of two opposite forces is their difference, in the direction of the larger force.

Activity 9.3 — Two parallel forces

Textbook · p.172

Show that the resultant of two parallel forces in the same direction is their sum.

🧰 You need
  • A wooden strip with three holes X, Y, Z
  • Three spring balances A, B, C
📝 Steps
  1. Hang spring balances A, B and C from holes X, Y and Z of the strip (Fig. 9.10).
  2. Pull the strip with the balances so that it stays at rest.
  3. Note the readings of A, B and C and compare them.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
When the strip is at rest, the reading of C equals the sum of the readings of A and B. C balances the resultant of A and B, so the resultant of two parallel forces in the same direction is their sum.

Take the 5 N direction as positive (+) and the opposite as negative (−).

= 3 N

Every practice question from the textbook — each with a full worked solution

Exercise 9.1Same direction

1(i).Find the resultant of 100 N and 200 N acting in the same direction.
100 + 200 = 300 N
1(ii).Find the resultant of 20 N and 40 N acting in the same direction.
20 + 40 = 60 N
2.One child pushes an object with 5 N while another pulls it in the same direction with 7 N. What is the resultant of these two forces?
5 + 7 = 12 N

Exercise 9.2Opposite direction

1.Two children push/pull a box: 15 N one way, 11 N the opposite way. Find the resultant of these two forces.
15 − 11 = 4 N
In the direction of the 15 N force.
2.An object on a table is given a 10 N force westward. It is also pulled by force X in the opposite (eastward) direction. If the resultant drags it with an effective 5 N, find the magnitude of X.
X wins (the object moves in X's direction), so X − 10 = 5
X = 5 + 10 = 15 N

Exercise 9.3Parallel forces

1.Two parallel threads pull a trolley with a resultant of 20 N. Thread A applies 12 N. Find the force applied by thread B.
A + B = 20 (parallel, same direction, so they add)
B = 20 − 12 = 8 N

⭐ Must-know cheat sheet

  • resultant force = the single force that has the same effect as all the forces together
  • same direction on one line: resultant = F₁ + F₂
  • opposite directions on one line: resultant = the difference, towards the bigger force
  • two non-parallel forces: the resultant points between them
  • forces are measured in newtons (N) with a spring balance

Chapter Summary & Key Terms

One last read before the exam!

📌 Summary — 4 key points

  1. A single force that produces the same effect as several forces acting together is called their resultant force.
  2. The resultant of two forces in the same direction along one line is the sum of the two forces.
  3. For two opposite forces along one line, the resultant equals the difference of the two forces and acts in the direction of the larger force.
  4. The resultant of two non-parallel forces acts in a direction between the two forces.
