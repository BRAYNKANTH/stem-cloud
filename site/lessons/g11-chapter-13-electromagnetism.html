13.1  Magnets and the magnetic field

There are two kinds of magnets: permanent magnets (the magnetism is a property of the material and stays) and electromagnets (magnetic only while a current flows through a coil). Big electromagnets lift scrap iron; magnets are in electric motors, magnetic cards, MRI machines and many instruments. Iron, steel and nickel are attracted by a magnet; plastic, wood, paper and rubber are not.

Around a magnet is a region where it has an influence: its magnetic field. We cannot see it, but it acts on other magnets and on moving charges (some birds use the Earth's field to navigate). A compass, a small light magnet free to turn on a pivot, shows the direction of the field at a point: it lines up north-south in the Earth's field. In Activity 13.1 only a magnet deflects the compass: glass, iron, plastic and brass do not.

The magnetic field around a bar magnet.

The needle of a compass shows the direction of the field at that point. The direction and the strength both change from point to point, so the field has a magnitude and a direction.

13.2  Magnetic effect of a current

When a current flows through a conductor, a magnetic field appears around it. The Danish scientist Hans Christian Oersted first noticed this. In Activity 13.2 a compass near a wire AB (north-south, above the compass) deflects when a current flows and returns when it stops; it deflects the opposite way if the compass is moved above the wire or the current is reversed. So the field is made by the current, and its direction depends on the direction of the current.

The field lines around a straight wire are circles.
Figures 13.10 and 13.11: finding the direction of the field.
Two rules for the field direction
Maxwell's corkscrew rule: turn a corkscrew so that its tip moves along the current; the direction it turns is the direction of the field lines. Right hand grip rule: hold the wire in your right hand with the thumb along the current; the fingers curling round the wire show the field direction.

A field coming out of the page is drawn as a dot in a circle (⊙) and a field going into the page as a cross in a circle (⊗). For a current along AB, by the right hand rule the field points out of the page above the wire and into the page below it.

13.3  Force on a current in a magnetic field

Place a wire BC on two rods between the poles of a U-magnet and pass a current (Activity 13.3): the wire moves. A force acts on a current carrying conductor in a magnetic field. Reverse the current, or swap the poles, and it moves the opposite way. The force is perpendicular to both the field and the current.

📏 The size of the force
  • It is directly proportional to the current, to the length of the conductor in the field and to the strength of the magnetic field.
Figure 13.14: Fleming's left hand rule.
Fleming's left hand rule
Hold the thumb, first finger and middle finger of the left hand at right angles. Point the first finger along the magnetic field and the middle finger along the current: the thumb shows the direction of the force.

This effect is the basis of electric motors, loud speakers, galvanometers and analog voltmeters and ammeters.

13.4  Loud speaker and DC motor

Figure 13.15: a loud speaker.

A loud speaker has a light cardboard cone, a coil fixed to the narrow end of the cone, and a ring magnet. When a current that varies like the sound waveform flows in the coil, the magnet exerts a varying force on it. The coil and the cone vibrate forwards and backwards and make sound waves. Electric energy becomes sound (via kinetic energy).

Figures 13.16 and 13.21: a simple DC motor.

A DC motor turns electric energy into mechanical energy. With the current flowing along AB and CD in the field, Fleming's left hand rule gives opposite forces on the two sides: a couple that turns the coil. In Activity 13.4 a coil of about 30 turns of copper wire sits on two needles on a dry cell with a ring magnet; the insulation is scraped off only on one half of the wire at each end so that current flows for half of each turn and the coil keeps turning one way.

  • Armature: the coil wound on a soft iron core; it turns when a current flows.
  • Magnetic poles: permanent magnets that give the field.
  • Commutator: two split rings turning with the coil and two fixed brushes touching them. Every half turn it reverses the current in the coil so that the couple always acts the same way and the coil keeps turning in one direction.

In the model of Figure 13.21 the current goes P → X → loop ABCD → Y → Q. The force on AB is downwards and on CD upwards, so the armature turns clockwise. After half a turn the split rings swap contacts, so the current flows D → C → B → A; the forces still turn the coil clockwise. Reversing the battery reverses the rotation. A stronger magnet gives a bigger force.

13.5  Electromagnetic induction

A current in a magnetic field makes a force. The opposite also happens: if a conductor moves in a magnetic field (or the field around it changes), an electromotive force (EMF) appears across its ends. This is electromagnetic induction, introduced by Michael Faraday (law presented in 1831). It is used in magnetic cards (door cards, credit and debit cards) and, above all, to generate the electricity we use from oil, coal and nuclear energy.

Figure 13.26 (Activity 13.5): moving a magnet towards a coil.
🧪 What Activity 13.5 shows
  • The galvanometer deflects only when the magnet and the coil move relative to each other (closer or further apart). If both are still, or move together without changing the gap, there is no deflection.
  • There is no battery, so the EMF is produced by the changing magnetic field lines linked with the coil: an induced EMF and an induced current.
  • The faster the magnet moves, the bigger the deflection: the EMF is proportional to the rate of change of the field lines.
Factors affecting the induced EMF
The induced EMF is bigger with (i) more turns in the coil, (ii) a stronger magnet, (iii) a faster motion of the magnet or the coil.
Fleming's right hand rule
For a straight conductor moving across a magnetic field: right hand, thumb, first finger and middle finger at right angles. Thumb = motion of the conductor, first finger = magnetic field, middle finger = induced current.

13.6  Dynamos, microphones and kinds of current

An AC dynamo has a coil ABCD with many turns rotating between two magnet poles. The ends of the coil go to two brushing (slip) rings, and two carbon brushes carry the current to the outside circuit. The arms AB and CD cut the field as it turns, so an EMF is induced. When AB moves up and CD down, Fleming's right hand rule gives a current A to B and C to D, so it flows round the coil in the direction ABCD; half a turn later it flows DCBA. The current changes direction every half turn.

Figure 13.29: an alternating EMF from a rotating coil.
  • When the coil is vertical (positions a, c, e) the arms move parallel to the field and cut no field lines: EMF = 0.
  • When the coil is horizontal (b and d, plane parallel to the field) the arms cut the field lines at right angles: EMF is maximum, to the left at b and to the right at d.
  • The EMF is a sine wave in time. In a dynamo, mechanical energy becomes electric energy.

In a moving coil microphone, sound makes the diaphragm and the light coil on it vibrate in a magnet's field. The field linked to the coil changes, so an alternating EMF is induced that follows the sound. A bicycle dynamo has a magnet turned by the rough head against the tyre, with a coil on soft iron (which links most of the field lines). The turning magnet keeps changing the field through the coil, so it gives an alternating current; the faster the bicycle goes, the faster the magnet turns, the larger the EMF and the brighter the lamp.

A current whose direction does not change with time is a direct current (DC): dry cells and solar cells give DC. A current whose direction changes with time is an alternating current (AC): the dynamo gives AC. Connect a centre-zero galvanometer to a battery and the needle stays on one side; connect it to a slowly turned AC dynamo and the needle swings left and right about zero.

13.7  Transformers

In Activity 13.6 two coils are wound on the same soft iron bundle. A battery and switch go to the first coil and a galvanometer to the second. When the switch is turned on, the galvanometer kicks one way; while the current flows steadily it reads zero; when turned off it kicks the opposite way; with no current it reads zero. The first coil makes a changing magnetic field that reaches the second coil through the iron, so an EMF is induced only while the field is changing. With an alternating voltage on the first coil the field changes all the time, so an alternating voltage is induced in the second coil. Such a pair of coils is a transformer: it works only with AC (or changing DC), never with steady DC.

Figures 13.36 and 13.37: a transformer.

The coil connected to the supply is the primary (Np turns, voltage Vp); the coil that supplies the load is the secondary (Ns turns, induced voltage Vs).

Transformer equations
Vp ÷ Vs = Np ÷ Ns    Vp Ip = Vs Is
the second equation assumes no energy loss (100 % efficiency): power in = power out
  • Step-up transformer: Ns is larger than Np, so Vs is larger than Vp.
  • Step-down transformer: Ns smaller than Np, so Vs is smaller than Vp.
Figure 13.39: transformers between the power station and the home.

Step-up transformers raise the generated voltage to 132 kV or 220 kV for the national grid; step-down transformers in sub-stations lower it for homes. Step-up transformers also give the high voltages in microwave ovens and X-ray tubes, and transformers are in power packs, computers and radios. Real transformers lose some energy as heat, but here we take the efficiency as 100 %.

Example
A transformer has 500 turns in the primary and 5000 in the secondary; 12 V is applied to the primary and 2 A flows in it. Find (i) Vs, (ii) Is, (iii) the type.
(i) Vs = 12 × 5000 ÷ 500 = 120 V
(ii) 12 × 2 = 120 × Is, so Is = 0.2 A
(iii) Vs is larger than Vp: a step-up transformer.

Activity 13.1: what deflects a compass?

Textbook · Activity 13.1, p.116

Show that a magnet makes a magnetic field.

🧰 You need
  • A compass
  • A piece of glass, iron, plastic, brass and a magnet
📝 Steps
  1. Place the compass on a table.
  2. Bring each object near it one at a time and watch the needle.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The needle deflects only for the magnet: a magnet makes a magnetic field around it.

Activity 13.2: a current makes a magnetic field

Textbook · Activity 13.2, p.118

Find that a current deflects a compass.

🧰 You need
  • A compass, a straight copper wire AB
  • Batteries, a switch, connecting wires
📝 Steps
  1. Put the compass on the table with the needle north-south and the wire AB above it, also north-south.
  2. Close the switch and note the deflection; open it.
  3. Place the compass above the wire and repeat; then reverse the battery and repeat.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The needle deflects only while a current flows; above and below the wire it deflects in opposite ways, and reversing the current reverses the deflection. A current produces a magnetic field whose direction depends on the current direction.

Activity 13.3: force on a current in a field

Textbook · Activity 13.3, p.121

See a current carrying wire move between magnet poles.

🧰 You need
  • A U-magnet, two brass rods, a conductor BC, two dry cells
  • A switch, thick cardboard
📝 Steps
  1. Set up the rods through the cardboard, connect the cells and switch, and lay the conductor BC across the rods between the poles.
  2. Close the switch and watch BC.
  3. Reverse the cells, then swap the magnet poles, and watch again.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
BC rolls along the rods (away from the magnet in the first case), the opposite way when the current or the poles are reversed. The force is perpendicular to both the field and the current.

Activity 13.4: build a simple DC motor

Textbook · Activity 13.4, p.124–125

Make a coil spin using a cell and a magnet.

🧰 You need
  • A dry cell, insulated copper wire, two needles with large holes
  • Clay, cello tape, a knife, a small ring magnet
📝 Steps
  1. Wind about 30 turns of wire round a pen, leaving long ends, and wind the ends round the coil.
  2. Scrape the insulation off one half of each end (the same side on both ends).
  3. Rest the ends on the two needles fixed to the cell terminals, stick the magnet on the cell and give the coil a small push.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The coil spins. The magnet's field exerts opposite forces on the two sides of the coil (a couple). Removing the insulation from only half keeps the current flowing for only half of each turn so the couple always turns the coil the same way.

Activity 13.5: induction with a magnet and a coil

Textbook · Activity 13.5, p.132–133

Find when a coil and a magnet make a current.

🧰 You need
  • A bar magnet, a thread-reel tube, about 1 m of gauge 28 copper wire
  • A centre-zero galvanometer
📝 Steps
  1. Wind the wire on the tube to make a coil and join its ends to the galvanometer.
  2. Move the magnet towards, away from, or keep it still near the coil; then move the coil instead; then move both together.
  3. Compare quick and slow movements.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
There is a deflection whenever the distance between the magnet and the coil changes, none when they are still or move together. A faster movement gives a larger deflection. An EMF is induced when the magnetic field through the coil changes.

Activity 13.6: two coils on one core

Textbook · Activity 13.6, p.142–143

See how a changing current in one coil induces a current in another.

🧰 You need
  • About 2 m of gauge 28 copper wire, a bundle of soft iron wires
  • Two dry cells, a centre-zero galvanometer, a switch
📝 Steps
  1. Wind about 100 turns on the iron bundle, and a second similar coil about 1 cm away.
  2. Join the cells and switch to one coil and the galvanometer to the other.
  3. Turn the switch on, leave it on, turn it off, and leave it off, watching the galvanometer each time.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
A kick when the switch goes on, zero while on, an opposite kick when it goes off, zero while off. An EMF is induced only while the current, and so the magnetic field, is changing: the principle of the transformer.

Worked Examples

Step-by-step solutions to the textbook examples
Textbook example · Ch.13, p.146
A transformer has 500 turns in its primary coil and 5000 in its secondary coil. 12 V is supplied to the primary. (i) Find the secondary voltage. (ii) If a current of 2 A passes through the primary, find the secondary current. (iii) Which type of transformer is it?

(i) Vp ÷ Vs = Np ÷ Ns, so Vs = 12 × 5000 ÷ 500 = 120 V.

(ii) Vp Ip = Vs Is: 12 × 2 = 120 × Is, so Is = 0.2 A.

(iii) The output voltage is larger than the input voltage.

Final answer: (i) 120 V, (ii) 0.2 A, (iii) a step-up transformer (the voltage rises, the current falls, the power stays 24 W).
Textbook exercise · Ch.13, Ex.13.5 Q1
A primary coil has 1000 turns and the secondary 100 turns. 230 V AC is supplied to the primary. Assume no energy loss. Find (i) the secondary voltage, (ii) the secondary current if the primary current is 5 A.

Vs = Vp × Ns ÷ Np = 230 × 100 ÷ 1000 = 23 V.

Is = Vp Ip ÷ Vs = 230 × 5 ÷ 23 = 50 A.

Final answer: 23 V and 50 A (step-down: lower voltage, larger current).
Practice example · power transmission
A power station gives 11 kV. A step-up transformer raises it to 220 kV. What is the ratio Ns ÷ Np, and by what factor does the current in the lines fall?

Ns ÷ Np = Vs ÷ Vp = 220 ÷ 11 = 20.

Vp Ip = Vs Is gives Is = Ip ÷ 20.

Final answer: the secondary has 20 times as many turns, and the current in the lines is 20 times smaller.

Practice Exercises

Every practice question from the textbook, each with a worked solution

Exercise 13.1Force on a conductor · p.121

1.Find and mark the direction of the force on the conductor using Fleming's left hand rule in each of the four figures (field between N and S poles, current out of or into the page).
The field points from N to S. (i) N left, S right, current out of the page (dot): force upwards. (ii) N left, S right, current into the page (cross): force downwards. (iii) S left, N right, current out: force downwards. (iv) S left, N right, current in: force upwards. (Reversing either the field or the current reverses the force.)

Exercise 13.2Fleming's rule, loud speaker, DC motor · p.130–131

1(i).For what purpose is Fleming's left hand rule used?
To find the direction of the force on a current carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field.
1(ii).What physical quantities do the fingers A, B and C in the figure represent?
A (first finger): the magnetic field; B (middle finger): the current; C (thumb): the force.
1(iii).Write down what happens to the wire in each of the four cases (a) to (d), using Fleming's left hand rule.
The wire moves sideways through the magnet, because the force is perpendicular to both the field and the current. Compare each case with (a): (b) the poles are swapped, so the wire moves the opposite way; (c) the battery is reversed, so it moves the opposite way; (d) both the poles and the battery are reversed, so it moves the same way as in (a). Reversing either the field or the current reverses the force; reversing both leaves it unchanged. (In (a) the arrangement is the one of Activity 13.3, where the wire is pushed along the rods away from the magnet.)
2(i).The figure shows the cross section of a loud speaker. Label A, B and C.
A: the (ring) magnet; B: the cardboard cone; C: the coil.
2(ii).Write down a special characteristic of the current entering at the terminal X.
It is an alternating (time-varying) current whose variation follows the waveform of the sound.
2(iii).Explain the operation of the loud speaker.
The varying current flows in the coil, which is in the field of the magnet. The magnet exerts a force on the current in the coil, and this force changes with the current. So the coil moves forward and backward, and the cone attached to it vibrates in the same way, making sound waves in the air.
2(iv).Write down the energy transformation in a loud speaker.
Electric energy → kinetic energy (of the coil and cone) → sound energy.
2(v).Write down the function of each of the parts A, B and C.
A (magnet) gives the magnetic field that exerts the force on the coil. B (cone) vibrates and pushes on the air to make the sound waves. C (coil) carries the varying current, so the magnet exerts a varying force and the coil moves.
3(i).The figure shows the main parts of a DC motor. Label P and Q.
P and Q: the brushes (carbon brushes that press against the split rings and connect the coil to the battery).
3(ii).What are the components labelled X and Y?
X and Y: the two halves of the split ring (the commutator).
3(iii).Indicate the direction of current flow in terms of the letters when switch K is closed.
As in Figure 13.21: from the battery into the brush, round to the split ring, along the coil A → B → C → D, to the other split ring, out through the other brush and back to the battery (P → X → A → B → C → D → Y → Q when P is joined to the positive terminal). Check which terminal of E is positive on your copy of the figure.
3(iv).What is the direction of rotation of the motor when K is closed?
Applying Fleming's left hand rule to the current and the field of the figure, as in Figure 13.21 the forces on the two sides of the coil are opposite and the armature turns clockwise (the direction of the couple). The direction follows the battery polarity: reversed polarity gives anticlockwise.
3(v).Write down the function of (a) V and U, (b) E, (c) P and Q, (d) X and Y.
(a) V and U, the magnetic poles (permanent magnets): produce the magnetic field. (b) E, the battery: supplies the current. (c) P and Q, the brushes: touch the split rings and carry the current between the battery and the coil. (d) X and Y, the split rings (commutator): reverse the current in the coil every half turn so that the coil keeps turning in one direction.
3(vi).What changes happen in the motor when (a) the battery terminals are interchanged, (b) the strength of the magnet is increased?
(a) The forces reverse, so the motor turns in the opposite direction. (b) The force on the coil increases, so the motor turns faster and with a stronger turning effect (it can drive a bigger load).

Exercise 13.3Direction of induced current · p.135

1.Using Fleming's right hand rule, find and mark the direction of the induced current in each of the eight instances (wire AB or the end-on wire between N and S poles, moving up or down).
Thumb = motion, first finger = field (N to S), middle finger = induced current. (i) current from A to B; (ii) poles swapped: B to A; (iii) motion reversed (up): B to A; (iv) both reversed: A to B. For the end-on wires: (v) N left, S right, moving up: current into the page (×); (vi) S left, N right, up: out of the page (⊙); (vii) N left, S right, down: out of the page (⊙); (viii) S left, N right, down: into the page (×). Reversing either the motion or the field reverses the current.

Exercise 13.4Direct and alternating current · p.141

1.Write down some instances where alternating currents and direct currents are used.
AC: household mains (230 V, 50 Hz) for bulbs, fans, irons; the output of a dynamo; transmission of electricity through the grid; the input of a transformer. DC: dry cells and batteries (torches, remote controls), solar cells, and the circuits of electronic devices such as radios and computers (after rectification).
2.Six graphs (a) to (f) of current against time are given. Say which type of current each represents and give reasons.
A current is direct if its direction never changes (the graph stays on one side of the time axis) and alternating if the direction changes (the graph crosses the axis). (a) direct: always positive, only the size varies. (b) direct: always positive (pulses, like a rectified current). (c) alternating: the graph goes above and below zero. (d) direct: always negative, so one direction. (e) alternating: a square wave that switches between positive and negative. (f) direct: always zero or negative, so the direction never reverses.

Exercise 13.5Transformers, induction and dynamos · p.148–150

Vs = Vp × Ns ÷ Np = 230 × 100 ÷ 1000 = 23 V
1(ii).Find the secondary current if the primary current is 5 A.
Vp Ip = Vs Is: 230 × 5 = 23 × Is
Is = 1150 ÷ 23 = 50 A

Vs = 230 × 500 ÷ 5000 = 23 V
2(ii).Find the current input of the primary if the current output of the secondary is 10 A.
230 × Ip = 23 × 10 = 230
Ip = 1 A
3(i).The ratio of primary to secondary turns is 1 : 10. 6 V AC is supplied to the primary and 20 A output current is needed (efficiency 100 %). Find the voltage output of the secondary.
Vs = Vp × Ns ÷ Np = 6 × 10 = 60 V
3(ii).Find the current input to the primary.
6 × Ip = 60 × 20 = 1200, so Ip = 200 A
3(iii).Find the ratio between the primary voltage and the secondary voltage.
Vp : Vs = 6 : 60 = 1 : 10 (the same as Np : Ns)
3(iv).Find the ratio between the primary current and the secondary current.
Ip : Is = 200 : 20 = 10 : 1 (the inverse of the voltage ratio)
4(i).Describe electromagnetic induction in a simple manner.
When a conductor moves in a magnetic field, or the magnetic field through a conductor or coil changes, an EMF is produced across its ends; if the circuit is closed an induced current flows.
4(ii).When the north pole of the bar magnet was brought quickly towards the coil, the centre-zero galvanometer deflected to the right. Does the current through G flow from A to B or from B to A?
The coil must oppose the approaching north pole, so the end of the coil nearest the magnet becomes a north pole: the current circulates anticlockwise as seen from the magnet. Following the winding of Figure 13.40, this makes the current flow through G from B to A. (This follows the standard direction rule for such a coil; check it on the winding of your own figure.)
4(iii).What is the direction of deflection of the galvanometer when the magnet is moved away from the coil?
To the left: the opposite direction, because the change of the field is reversed.
4(iv).What is the direction of deflection if the south pole of the bar magnet is moved towards the coil?
To the left: a south pole approaching gives the opposite current to a north pole approaching.
4(v).Write down three factors that the strength of the current through the galvanometer depends on.
(1) The number of turns of the coil; (2) the strength of the magnet; (3) the speed with which the magnet (or the coil) is moved.
5(i).The figure shows the internal components of a bicycle dynamo. Label A, B, C and D.
A: the rough head; B: the (cylindrical) magnet; C: the soft iron; D: the coil (wound round the soft iron).
5(ii).What principle is the operation of a dynamo based on?
Electromagnetic induction.
5(iii).Describe the operation of the dynamo.
The rough head touches the tyre and turns when the wheel turns, so the magnet turns too. The magnetic field linked with the coil (wound on the soft iron) keeps changing, so an EMF is induced in the coil; the lamp connected to x and y lights.
5(iv).Is the output of a dynamo a direct current or an alternating current?
An alternating current: the magnet's field through the coil keeps reversing.
5(v).Draw a rough sketch of the variation of the voltage output with time.
A sine wave, positive and negative about zero:
5(vi).The brightness of the lamp depends on the speed of the bicycle. Explain.
Riding faster makes the head and the magnet turn faster, so the magnetic field through the coil changes faster. The induced EMF is proportional to the rate of change of the field, so it is larger, the current is larger and the lamp is brighter.
5(vii).Write down the energy transformation when the lamp is lit by the dynamo.
Kinetic (mechanical) energy of the turning magnet → electric energy → light (and heat) energy.
6.The figure shows a moving coil galvanometer. Name the components A, B, C and D and describe the function of each.
Reading the figure: C, the permanent magnet (with curved poles): gives a strong magnetic field. B, the coil (wound on a soft iron core): the current to be measured flows through it, the magnet exerts forces on its two sides and the coil turns. A, the pointer with its scale: moves with the coil and shows the deflection, which is larger for a larger current. D, the spring and terminals (the leads): carry the current into the coil and give a restoring force that brings the coil and pointer back to zero. Check the labels against your copy of the figure.

📌 Summary — 9 key points

  1. A current carrying conductor has a magnetic field round it; its direction is found with the corkscrew rule or the right hand grip rule.
  2. A force acts on a current carrying conductor in a magnetic field; it is proportional to the current, the length in the field and the field strength.
  3. Fleming's left hand rule (first finger field, middle finger current, thumb force) gives the direction of the force.
  4. DC motors and loud speakers work by this force; in a motor, electric energy becomes mechanical energy.
  5. Electromagnetic induction: an EMF is induced when the magnetic field through a circuit changes; it depends on the turns, the magnet strength and the speed.
  6. Fleming's right hand rule (thumb motion, first finger field, middle finger current) gives the direction of the induced current.
  7. The bicycle dynamo, the moving coil microphone and the transformer work by induction; a dynamo changes mechanical energy into electric energy.
  8. Direct current keeps its direction; alternating current changes it. Cells give DC, a dynamo gives AC.
  9. A transformer changes an AC voltage: Vp ÷ Vs = Np ÷ Ns and Vp Ip = Vs Is.
