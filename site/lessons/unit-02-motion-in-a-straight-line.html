2.1  Distance vs. Displacement

When you travel from one place to another, you might take a longer or shorter route — a winding road, a shortcut, whatever. The total length of the path you actually walked is your distance. But no matter which route you took, you end up a certain straight-line distance from where you started, in a certain direction — that's your displacement.

Distance only tells you "how much ground was covered." Displacement tells you "where you ended up, relative to where you started, and in which direction." That's why distance is a scalar (magnitude only) and displacement is a vector (magnitude + direction).

Definitions
Scalar = magnitude only  ·  Vector = magnitude + direction
Scalars: distance, speed, time, mass  |  Vectors: displacement, velocity, acceleration, weight

2.2 – 2.3  Speed vs. Velocity

Speed is simply how fast distance is being covered — distance travelled per unit time. It doesn't care about direction, so it's a scalar. If an object covers equal distances in equal time intervals, it is moving at a uniform (constant) speed. If not, we describe its mean (average) speed over the whole journey.

Velocity is the same idea, but for displacement instead of distance — the rate of change of displacement. Because displacement has a direction, velocity is a vector. A body moving at constant velocity keeps both its speed and its direction unchanged.

Speed (scalar)
speed = distance ÷ time
Velocity (vector)
velocity = displacement ÷ time
Mean / average speed
= total distance ÷ total time
Displacement (uniform velocity)
displacement = velocity × time

2.4  Acceleration

Real journeys rarely stay at one constant velocity — vehicles speed up, slow down, and change direction. Acceleration measures how quickly velocity itself is changing: the change in velocity per unit time.

If velocity increases, acceleration is positive. If velocity decreases, acceleration is negative — this is called deceleration. A body whose velocity changes by the same amount every second has a uniform acceleration.

Acceleration
a = (final v − initial v) ÷ time
Unit: m s-2. A negative result means the body is decelerating.
Displacement (uniform acceleration)
s = mean velocity × time
mean velocity = (initial v + final v) ÷ 2

2.5 – 2.6  Reading Motion Graphs

A displacement–time graph plots position against time. If the line is straight, velocity is uniform; if it curves, velocity is changing. Either way, the gradient (slope) of the graph at any point gives the velocity at that instant.

A velocity–time graph plots velocity against time. Its gradient gives the acceleration. A flat, horizontal line means constant velocity (zero acceleration). Even more useful: the area enclosed between the line and the time-axis gives the displacement — this works whether the motion is uniform (a rectangle), uniformly accelerating (a triangle or trapezium), or a mix of phases (add up the area of each phase).

s–t graph
gradient = velocity
v–t graph
gradient = acceleration
v–t graph — the big one
area under the line = displacement

2.7  Gravitational Acceleration (Free Fall)

Every falling object speeds up as it falls, because the Earth's gravity accelerates it downward. This is the gravitational acceleration, symbol g. Near sea level, g ≈ 9.8 m s-2 (often rounded to 10 m s-2 for easier calculation) — meaning a freely falling object gains about 9.8 m s-1 of downward velocity every second.

Throw something straight up instead, and gravity works against its motion: its upward velocity decreases by about 9.8 m s-1 every second, i.e. the acceleration is −9.8 m s-2 (taking "upward" as positive), until it momentarily stops at its highest point.

Gravitational acceleration
g ≈ 9.8 m s-2 (down)  ·  −9.8 m s-2 for a body moving upward

⚠️ Common mistakes
  • Distance ≠ Displacement. Distance is the total path length walked (always positive, no direction). Displacement is the straight-line change in position (can be zero, or "negative" relative to a chosen direction). Don't use them interchangeably in an answer.
  • Dropping the negative sign. A negative acceleration doesn't mean "no acceleration" — it means the object is decelerating. Keep the sign through your working, then state the deceleration as a positive magnitude at the end if asked for one.
  • Mixing up gradient and area. On a displacement–time graph, the gradient gives velocity. On a velocity–time graph, the area gives displacement (its gradient gives acceleration instead). Confusing the two is one of the most common exam errors.
  • Treating a multi-stage journey as one stage. When a body speeds up, then moves at constant velocity, then slows down, calculate the displacement of each phase separately and add them — never apply one formula across the whole journey.
  • Missing units. Always write m, m s-1, or m s-2 as appropriate — O/L markers deduct marks for a correct number with no (or wrong) unit.

A child rides a bicycle on a straight path. Her displacement from the starting point, measured every 1 s, is given below.

s (m)02468888840
(i) What kind of motion occurred during the first 4 s?  (ii) Find the rate of change of displacement during the first 4 s.  (iii) Give one word for this rate of change.  (iv) Describe the motion from 4 s to 8 s.  (v) Describe the motion from 8 s to 10 s.  (vi) Find the velocity during the last 2 s.

(i) From t = 0 to 4 s, displacement rises steadily by 2 m every second (0 → 2 → 4 → 6 → 8 m).

The child moved forward 8 m at a uniform (constant) velocity.
Mark for: spotting that equal displacement occurs in equal time intervals — the definition of uniform velocity.

(ii) Rate of change of displacement = velocity = (final s − initial s) ÷ time

= (8 − 0) m ÷ 4 s = 2 m s-1
Where marks are lost: forgetting the unit m s-1, or using the wrong time interval.

(iii) The rate of change of displacement is called velocity.

Mark for: correct terminology — not "speed."

(iv) From 4 s to 8 s, displacement stays fixed at 8 m — it does not change.

The child was not moving (at rest) during this interval.
Where marks are lost: saying "moving at constant velocity" — a flat line means zero velocity, not constant nonzero velocity.

(v) From 8 s to 10 s, displacement falls from 8 m back to 0 m.

The child travelled back in the opposite direction, arriving back at the starting point at t = 10 s.
Mark for: noting the direction reversed, not just that displacement "decreased."

(vi) Velocity during the last 2 s = (final s − initial s) ÷ time = (0 − 8) m ÷ 2 s

= −4 m s-1
Where marks are lost: dropping the minus sign — it's what tells you the motion is in the opposite direction.
Final answers: (i) uniform velocity forward  (ii) 2 m s-1  (iii) velocity  (iv) at rest  (v) moved back to the start  (vi) −4 m s-1, i.e. 4 m s-1 in the opposite direction.
Worked Example · Unit 2, p.34–35
A body starting from rest accelerates for 4 s and reaches a velocity of 12 m s-1. It then moves at a constant 12 m s-1 for 4 s, and finally decelerates to rest over the last 2 s.
(i) Find the acceleration during the first 4 s. (ii) Find the deceleration during the last 2 s. (iii) Find the total displacement over the 10 s.

(i) Acceleration = (final v − initial v) ÷ time = (12 − 0) m s-1 ÷ 4 s

= 3 m s-2
Marks for: the correct formula (1) and correct substitution with unit (1).

(ii) Acceleration during the last 2 s = (0 − 12) m s-1 ÷ 2 s = −6 m s-2

Deceleration = 6 m s-2
Where marks are lost: giving −6 as the final answer to "find the deceleration" — deceleration is usually reported as a positive magnitude.

Displacement in first 4 s = mean velocity × time = (0 + 12)/2 × 4

= 24 m
Marks for: using mean velocity for the accelerating phase, not final velocity alone.

Displacement in next 4 s (constant velocity) = velocity × time = 12 × 4

= 48 m

Displacement in last 2 s = mean velocity × time = (12 + 0)/2 × 2

= 12 m

(iii) Total displacement = 24 + 48 + 12

= 84 m
Where marks are lost: forgetting one of the three phases, or using 10 s with a single average-velocity formula (only valid for a single uniform-acceleration phase).
Final answers: acceleration = 3 m s-2, deceleration = 6 m s-2, total displacement = 84 m.
Worked Example · Unit 2, p.42–43
An object starts from rest and reaches 15 m s-1 after 6 s of uniform acceleration. It then travels at that velocity for 6 s, before decelerating to rest over the final 3 s.
(ii) Acceleration during the first 6 s. (iii) Displacement during the first 6 s. (iv) Displacement at uniform velocity. (v) Deceleration during the last 3 s. (vi) Displacement during the last 3 s. (vii) Total displacement over the whole motion.

Acceleration (0 → A) = gradient of OA = (15 − 0) m s-1 ÷ 6 s

= 2.5 m s-2

Displacement under OA = area of triangle = ½ × 15 × 6

= 45 m
Where marks are lost: forgetting the ½ — this segment is a triangle, not a rectangle.

Displacement under AB (uniform velocity) = 15 × 6

= 90 m

Acceleration (B → C) = (0 − 15) m s-1 ÷ 3 s = −5 m s-2

Deceleration = 5 m s-2

Displacement under BC = ½ × 15 × 3

= 22.5 m

(i) Velocity falls by g = 10 m s-1 every second (taking "upward" as positive):

t (s)0123

Where marks are lost: adding 10 instead of subtracting — gravity opposes upward motion, so velocity decreases.

(ii) The graph is a straight line from (0, 30) down to (3, 0) — a constant (uniform) deceleration.

(iii) Maximum height = area under the graph = ½ × base × height = ½ × 3 s × 30 m s-1

Worked Example · Unit 2, p.36
An object starts from rest and reaches a velocity of 12 m s⁻¹ in 6 s with uniform acceleration. What is its displacement in that time?
Uniform acceleration means we can use the mean velocity: mean velocity = (0 + 12) ÷ 2 = 6 m s⁻¹.
displacement = mean velocity × time = 6 m s⁻¹ × 6 s = 36 m.
Final answer: The displacement is 36 m.
Worked Example · Unit 2, p.43
An object moving at 8 m s⁻¹ is brought to rest in 4 s by a uniform deceleration. Draw the velocity–time graph and find the displacement in the 4 s.
The graph is a straight line from (0 s, 8 m s⁻¹) down to (4 s, 0). The area under it is a triangle.
displacement = area = ½ × 4 s × 8 m s⁻¹ = (8 ÷ 2) × 4 = 16 m.
Final answer: The displacement is 16 m.

Exercise 2.1Distance and displacement · p.29

1.A child starts at A, walks 40 m east to B, then 30 m north to C (a right angle at B, AC = 50 m). What is (a) the distance the child travelled, (b) the child's displacement?
(a) distance = 40 m + 30 m = 70 m
(b) displacement = AC = √(40² + 30²) = 50 m, in the direction from A to C (about 37° north of east)

Exercise 2.2Acceleration · p.38

1.The velocity of an object changes uniformly from 0 to 12 m s⁻¹ in 6 s. Find its acceleration.
a = (12 − 0) ÷ 6 = 2 m s⁻²

a = (4 − 16) ÷ 4 = −3 m s⁻²
deceleration = 3 m s⁻²

Chapter Summary & Key Terms

One last read before the exam!

📌 Summary — 6 key points

  1. The distance moved by a moving object is given by the total length of its path.
  2. The quantity that fixes the change in position of a moving object is its displacement: it needs the straight-line distance between the start and the end, and the direction. Displacement is a vector; distance has only magnitude and is a scalar.
  3. Speed is the distance moved in unit time; it is a scalar.
  4. Velocity is the rate of change of displacement. Since it has direction as well as magnitude, it is a vector. velocity = displacement ÷ time.
  5. Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. The displacement equals the area enclosed between the velocity–time graph and the time axis.
  6. A negative acceleration is a retardation (deceleration).
