Friction

Why is it hard to walk on ice? Why do bicycle wheels have ball bearings? Why is driving on worn-out tires dangerous in the rain? The answer to all of these is friction — the force that resists one surface sliding against another. Deep explanations, lots of examples, below.

In Sector 1, you saw surfaces resisting motion. Below are the official laws of friction, limiting friction formulas, and practical calculations!

5.1  What is Friction, and Where Does It Come From?

Roll a ball along the ground — it travels a bit, slows down gradually, and finally stops, right? We have all seen an object moving over a rough surface lose speed little by little and come to rest. The reason: the rough surface opposes the object's motion!

Try pushing a heavy bookshelf across the floor — it's tough, right? That difficulty has a name: friction. When two surfaces are in contact and one tries to slide (or actually slides) over the other, a force arises from those surfaces that opposes that sliding motion — that's the friction force.

Here's the secret — no matter how "smooth" something looks, no surface is 100% perfectly flat. At the microscopic level, every surface has tiny bumps and dips invisible to the eye. Put two surfaces together, and these tiny bumps catch on each other, resisting the slide — that's exactly where friction comes from. That's why rougher surfaces produce more friction than smoother ones.

🔎 Feel it for yourself
  • Rub your palm slowly on a table — does it warm up? That heat is energy produced by friction! Friction always produces heat — that's exactly why the old method of rubbing two sticks together makes fire.

5.2  Three Types of Friction

Take a heavy wooden block on a table, and pull it with a spring balance, little by little. Pull gently at first, and the block doesn't move — but your pulling force isn't wasted either, because friction "matches" it exactly. This is the first type.

1️⃣ Static Friction

  • Acts when a force is applied but the object still hasn't started moving.
  • A "self-adjusting" force — it grows to exactly match however much you pull, up to a limit.

2️⃣ Limiting Friction

  • The maximum static friction — right at the instant the object is just about to start moving.
  • Only once your force exceeds this value does the object actually start moving.

3️⃣ Dynamic (Kinetic) Friction

  • The friction that acts on an object while it is already moving, opposing that motion.
  • Slightly less than limiting friction — that's exactly why it's harder to start moving a heavy object than to keep it moving once it's already sliding!

5.3  What Affects Limiting Friction — and What Doesn't

The textbook investigates this with three experiments. Let's look at each.

Factor 1 — Nature of the Surfaces
The rougher the surfaces, the more friction. Smooth, polished surfaces produce less friction.

Pulling the same block over sandpapers of different roughness, the rougher paper makes the spring balance read a higher value — meaning more force (more friction) is needed.

Factor 2 — Normal Reaction (Weight)
As an object's weight increases, the normal reaction from the surface below also increases — and so does the limiting friction.

A simple example — moving an empty box is easy, but fill it with stones to make it heavy, and it's much harder to push — weight went up, so friction went up too. In the textbook's experiment (Activity 5.4), stacking more and more blocks (each 20 N) on top and pulling them shows the required limiting friction force steadily increasing.

😲 Surprising fact — surface area does NOT matter!
  • Pull the same block on its long side, its short side, or its thick side — the limiting friction stays exactly the same! It doesn't matter whether the contact area is big or small — this is something students commonly get wrong.
What limiting friction depends on
Depends on: nature of surfaces (roughness), normal reaction (weight)  |  Does NOT depend on: contact surface area

5.4  Friction in Daily Life — Reducing It, and Using It

Friction always wastes energy (as heat) — so machines use several methods to reduce it.

🔧 Ways to reduce friction

  • Polishing/smoothing the surface — reduces roughness.
  • Using lubricants (oil, grease) — creates a slippery layer between the surfaces.
  • Using ball or roller bearings — rolling friction is much less than sliding friction. Used in bicycle wheel axles and to move heavy wood in carpentry.

✅ Where friction actually helps us

  • Walking — the tread pattern on shoe soles increases friction and stops us from slipping.
  • Vehicle tyres — give grip on the road; the tread grooves channel rainwater away.
  • Twisting a rope — the friction between fibres (jute or nylon) is what holds the rope together. It's also why knots hold.
  • Brakes — a bicycle's rubber pad pressing on the rim, or a car's disc brake pads pressed on the disc by a piston — both use friction's resistance to stop the vehicle.
1 / 4
⚠️ Common mistakes
  • Surface area does not affect friction! Whether the object rests on its large face or small face, limiting friction stays the same. Don't wrongly assume "bigger area = more friction."
  • Static friction is not always at its maximum. It matches whatever force you apply exactly — it only increases up to the limiting friction value.
  • Kinetic (dynamic) friction is slightly less than limiting friction. That's why a heavy object is hardest to start moving, but slightly easier to keep sliding once it has started.
  • Friction is not always harmful. Walking, gripping, and braking are all impossible without it — don't treat it as something to be "eliminated entirely"; it should be reduced or increased depending on the situation.

Activity 5.1 — When does the block finally move?

Textbook · p.105

See how friction behaves while a block is NOT moving, and find the exact pull at which it just starts to move.

🧰 You need
  • A spring balance (newton balance)
  • A wooden block of weight 60 N, with a small ring fixed to it
  • A flat, horizontal table
📝 Steps
  1. Fix a small ring on the block and hook the spring balance to it.
  2. Place the block on the horizontal table.
  3. Pull the balance very gently, horizontally. Read the force. The block stays still — this pull is too small to move it.
  4. Increase the pull little by little. At some moment the block just starts to move. Note the reading at that exact moment.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
While the block stays still, friction (static friction) exactly matches your pull — it grows as you pull more. The reading at the instant the block just starts to move is the LIMITING FRICTION: the biggest static friction these two surfaces can give.

Activity 5.2 — Does roughness matter?

Textbook · p.107

Find out how the nature (roughness) of the touching surfaces changes limiting friction.

🧰 You need
  • A wooden block of weight 60 N
  • A spring balance
  • Several sandpapers of different roughness
📝 Steps
  1. Wrap one sandpaper completely around the bottom face of the block.
  2. Place the block on the table with the sandpaper touching the table. Pull gently with the spring balance, then increase the pull little by little.
  3. Note the reading at the moment the block just starts to move. That is the limiting friction.
  4. Repeat with sandpapers of different roughness, and compare your readings.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The rougher the sandpaper, the bigger the limiting friction. Surface roughness DOES affect friction.

Activity 5.3 — Does contact area matter?

Textbook · p.108–109

Test whether the size of the contact area changes limiting friction.

🧰 You need
  • A 60 N wooden block with faces of different sizes (length, width and height all different)
  • A spring balance
  • Sandpapers of the SAME roughness
📝 Steps
  1. Cover each face of the block (different areas) with sandpaper of the same roughness.
  2. Place the block on the table on its LARGEST face and find the limiting friction as before.
  3. Turn the block onto each of its other faces and find the limiting friction each time.
  4. Compare the readings for all the faces.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The limiting friction is the SAME whichever face is on the table. Contact area does NOT affect limiting friction.

Activity 5.4 — Does weight matter?

Textbook · p.110

See how the weight of the object (the normal reaction) changes limiting friction.

🧰 You need
  • Three wooden blocks, each of weight 20 N
  • A spring balance
📝 Steps
  1. Place one 20 N block on the table and find its limiting friction with the spring balance.
  2. Put a second 20 N block on top of the first and find the limiting friction again (total weight 40 N).
  3. Put the third block on top as well and repeat (total weight 60 N).
  4. Compare the three readings.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The more weight you stack, the bigger the normal reaction R from the table — and the bigger the limiting friction. The readings rise step by step.

Concept Walkthroughs

Step-by-step reasoning for real textbook questions — friction has no formulas to plug in, but needs deep understanding!
Textbook Question · Ch.5, p.115
Why is it dangerous to drive a vehicle with worn-out tyre treads on rainy days?

What do tread grooves do? — Normally, the tyre and road need direct contact for good grip. When it rains, a film of water forms on the road — the grooves push that water sideways and out of the way, letting the tyre touch the road directly.

What happens once the treads wear out? — There's no way for the water to escape anymore. A thin film of water stays trapped between the tyre and the road — the tyre never actually touches the road directly!

Key idea: water has very low friction (it's very slippery). So as long as that thin film is there, friction drops sharply.
Final answer: Worn treads can't push away the water, so a film of water forms between tyre and road, cutting friction drastically — the vehicle can skid and lose grip and control, which is dangerous.
Concept Walkthrough 2
Why is it hard to get a heavy almirah started moving, but relatively easier to keep it moving once it has started sliding?

Before it starts moving: as long as the almirah is still, you need to apply a force exceeding the limiting friction — the maximum resistance the contact surfaces can offer.

Once it starts moving: now it's dynamic (kinetic) friction that acts — and this is slightly less than limiting friction.

Key idea: force needed to "start" it (limiting friction) > force needed to "keep it going" (kinetic friction). That's why it suddenly feels like the resistance dropped once it starts sliding!
Final answer: Starting motion requires exceeding limiting friction (needs more force); once moving, only dynamic friction resists, which is less than limiting friction — so keeping it moving is easier.
Textbook Question · Ch.5, p.115
Explain two methods used to reduce friction.

Method 1 — Lubricant: pour oil or grease between two surfaces, and they no longer touch directly — they "float" on the lubricant layer instead. A liquid layer's friction is much lower than solid-surface-to-solid-surface friction.

Method 2 — Bearings: rolling friction is much lower than sliding friction. Fit ball or roller bearings, and an axle "rolls" instead of "sliding" — friction drops substantially.

Remember: polishing the surface smooth is a third method — it directly reduces the rough bumps.
Final answer: (1) Using lubricants (oil/grease) — a liquid layer prevents direct surface-to-surface contact. (2) Using ball/roller bearings — rolling instead of sliding, greatly reducing friction.

ExerciseCh. 5, p.115

1(i).Briefly define friction.
Friction is the force exerted by two contacting surfaces on each other, opposing relative motion (or attempted relative motion) between them.
1(ii).Briefly define static friction.
The friction that acts, equal and opposite to the applied force, when a force is applied to an object but it still does not move.
1(iii).Briefly define limiting friction.
The maximum static friction, acting at the exact instant the object is just about to start moving.
1(iv).In what situation does dynamic (kinetic) friction act?
It acts on a moving object, opposing its motion — that is, while relative motion (actual sliding) is taking place.
1(v).What are the two main factors that affect limiting friction?
(1) The nature of the contact surfaces (rough / smooth)   (2) The normal reaction (depends on weight)
1(vi).What factor does NOT affect friction?
The area of the contact surfaces.
2(i).Give two advantages of friction.
(1) It stops us from slipping while walking. (2) It lets vehicles grip the road and stop when brakes are applied (also why rope knots hold).
2(ii).Write two harmful effects of friction.
(1) Wastes energy as heat. (2) Wears out machine parts, shoe soles, and tyres, shortening their lifespan.
2(iii).Why is it dangerous to drive vehicles with worn-out tyre treads on rainy days?
Treads need to clear water away from the surface — once worn, a thin film of water stays trapped between tyre and road, sharply cutting friction. This raises the risk of skidding and accidents (see Concept Walkthrough 1 above for the full explanation).
2(iv).Write two methods used to reduce friction.
(1) Using lubricants (oil, grease). (2) Using ball/roller bearings (see Concept Walkthrough 3 above for the full explanation).

Chapter Summary & Key Terms

One last read before the exam!

📌 Summary — 6 key points

  1. Friction is the force exerted by the touching surfaces that opposes the motion when one of two objects in contact moves (or tries to move) over the other.
  2. Static friction between two surfaces is different from a force applied from outside — it is the surfaces' own reaction, matching the applied force up to a limit.
  3. The force that must be applied to just start an object moving equals the limiting friction.
  4. Limiting friction depends on two things: (1) the nature of the touching surfaces, (2) the normal reaction.
  5. The area of the contact surfaces does NOT affect limiting friction.
  6. The friction acting on an object that is already moving is dynamic friction.
