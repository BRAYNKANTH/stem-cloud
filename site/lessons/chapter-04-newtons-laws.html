Newton's Laws of Motion

Why do you lurch backward when a stationary bus suddenly starts? Why is a loaded cart harder to push than an empty one? Isaac Newton answered these with three laws of motion — plus momentum, and the difference between mass and weight. Deep explanations, lots of worked examples, and practice exercises below.

4.1a  What is Force, and What Does It Do?

When you kick a ball, push a door, or lift a bag — do you know what you're actually doing? You're applying a force! A push or a pull, in the simplest sense, is a force. You can't see a force directly, but you can always see what it does.

A force acting on an object can do four things: (1) start a body that is at rest, (2) stop a body that is moving, (3) change the speed of a moving body (speed it up or slow it down), or (4) change its direction (like a cricket ball changing direction when hit by a bat). Sometimes a force also changes an object's shape — squeeze a rubber ball, and it deforms.

But just applying a force doesn't always mean an object starts moving! Try pushing gently on a heavy table — it won't budge. Why? Because friction from the floor pushes back against you with an equal opposing force. As long as the force you apply and the opposing friction force are balanced, they cancel out — no motion starts. Only when your force grows bigger than friction does the table finally move. What decides "does it move or not" is whether there's an unbalanced force acting.

Remember
Motion only starts or changes when there is an unbalanced (net) force
Think of a bullock pulling a cart, or people pushing a stalled car — everyone is trying to create this unbalanced force.

4.1b  Newton's First Law — the Law of Laziness (Inertia)

Here's a secret — every object has a bit of "laziness" built into it! Something at rest wants to stay at rest, and something moving wants to keep moving at the same speed and direction, until a force comes along and makes it change. This "resistance to change" is called inertia.

Newton's First Law
Unless an unbalanced force acts on it, a body at rest stays at rest, and a body moving with uniform velocity keeps moving with that same uniform velocity.

Want to see it directly? Think of a carrom board game. The powder sprinkled on it reduces friction. Flick the striker with your finger, and it slides a long way — because there's almost no friction force trying to stop it. That "keeps going" tendency is inertia. Same reason you lurch forward when a bus brakes suddenly — your body "wants" to keep moving even though the bus has stopped!

🚌 Sudden brake — you lurch forward

  • The bus is moving forward at speed.
  • It brakes suddenly — the bus stops.
  • But your body still "wants" the forward speed — so you tip forward.

🚌 Sudden start — you're pushed back

  • The bus is at rest, and so are you.
  • It suddenly starts moving forward.
  • Your body still "wants" to stay at rest — so you feel pushed backward.

This is exactly why wearing a seatbelt matters — in a crash, the car stops suddenly, but your body still "wants" to keep moving forward (inertia!). Without a seatbelt, you'd fly forward into the steering wheel or windscreen. The seatbelt holds you safely and controls that forward motion.

4.1c  Newton's Second Law — F = ma

The first law tells us when motion starts or changes. The second law tells us how much it changes — specifically, how much acceleration results from applying a force.

Think of two things: (1) For the same mass, a bigger force gives a bigger acceleration — so acceleration is directly proportional to force (a ∝ F). (2) For the same force, a heavier object accelerates less — so acceleration is inversely proportional to mass (a ∝ 1/m). A perfect everyday example — pushing an empty cart is easy, but the same cart loaded with heavy goods is hard to push — more mass means less acceleration for the same push.

Combine the two: a ∝ F/m. Add a constant of proportionality, so F/m ÷ a = a constant. The unit of force is defined so that this constant becomes exactly 1 — that is, the force needed to give a 1 kg mass an acceleration of 1 ms⁻² is defined as one newton (N). That makes F/m ÷ a = 1, i.e. F = ma — this small, powerful equation is Newton's Second Law!

Newton's Second Law
The acceleration produced in a body is directly proportional to the unbalanced force applied to it, and inversely proportional to the mass of the body.
Newton's Second Law (equation)
F = m × a
F = force (N)  |  m = mass (kg)  |  a = acceleration (ms-2). 1 N = 1 kg × 1 ms-2 = 1 kg ms-2

This one equation can be used three ways — whichever value is unknown, find it from the other two: Need F? F = ma. Need a? a = F/m. Need m? m = F/a. We'll work through all three in the Examples section below.

4.1d  Newton's Third Law — Action and Reaction

Newton's Third Law
For every action (force), there is an equal and opposite reaction (force).

This means — when you push something, it pushes you back, equally and in the opposite direction! Hard to believe? Think of a balloon — when you let go of an inflated balloon, air rushes out one way (the action), so the balloon shoots off the other way (the reaction)! The same principle sends rockets into space.

More examples — a rower pushes the water backward with the oar (the action), and the water pushes the boat forward (the reaction) — so the boat moves forward. Swimmers push water backward with their hands, and the water pushes them forward. If two kids on skateboards push against each other, both get pushed apart in opposite directions!

💡 Important to notice
  • Action and reaction always act on two different objects — never on the same one! The rower acts on the water; the water reacts on the boat.
  • That's why they don't cancel out and leave everything motionless — since they act on different objects, each produces its own separate effect.

4.2  Momentum — Mass and Velocity Together

Picture two balls moving at the same speed — a small table-tennis ball and a heavy bowling ball. Both move at the same speed, but which one is harder to stop? The heavy one — because it has more mass. Now roll the same ball at two different speeds — the faster one is harder to stop. So what decides "how hard it is to stop" is mass and velocity together. This combination is called momentum.

Momentum
momentum = mass × velocity  (p = m × v)
Unit: kg ms-1 (since mass is in kg and velocity in ms-1)

A fast-moving motor vehicle has a lot of momentum — that's exactly why it needs a longer distance and more time to stop. As speed decreases gradually, momentum decreases; as speed increases, momentum increases too.

🔗 A deeper connection (bonus)
  • Newton's Second Law can be stated even more generally as: force is the rate of change of momentum (F = Δp / Δt). For a constant mass, this works out to exactly F = ma. You can see this live in the Force Lab below!

4.3  Mass and Weight — Not the Same Thing!

Every day we say "weight" — but scientifically there are two separate ideas hiding in that word: mass and weight. Mixing these up is one of the most common mistakes — read carefully!

⚖️ Mass

  • The amount of matter contained in an object.
  • Unit: kg (kilogram)
  • Stays the same no matter where you are (Earth, Moon, space) — never changes.
  • It's a scalar quantity.

🌍 Weight

  • The gravitational force pulling an object toward the centre of the Earth (or any planet).
  • Unit: N (newton) — it's a force!
  • Changes from place to place — as gravity changes, weight changes.
  • It's a vector quantity — always directed downward.

Applying Newton's Second Law (F = ma) to weight, using the gravitational acceleration (g): Weight (W) = mass (m) × gravitational acceleration (g). On Earth, g ≈ 10 ms-2 (for easy calculation) — so a 1 kg mass has a weight of 10 N.

Weight
W = m × g
On Earth g ≈ 10 ms-2  |  On the Moon, gravity is only 1/6th of Earth's!

This leads to something amazing — take a 3 kg object to the Moon, and its mass is still 3 kg (the matter hasn't changed). But since gravity on the Moon is only 1/6 of Earth's, its weight there is only 1/6 of what it was on Earth. That's exactly why astronauts can jump so easily on the Moon — their mass didn't change, but their weight did!

1 / 6
⚠️ Common mistakes
  • Mass ≠ Weight. Everyday speech saying "weight is 60 kg" is technically wrong (kg is the unit of mass) — correctly: "mass is 60 kg," "weight is 600 N." Don't mix up the units (kg vs N) in an exam.
  • All three values in F = ma need the right units — mass always in kg (divide by 1000 if given in grams!), acceleration in ms-2, and force comes out in N.
  • Action and reaction never cancel on one object. They act on two different objects — don't wrongly assume "two opposite forces on the same object" and conclude "so it won't move."
  • Momentum is a vector quantity — because velocity has direction, momentum does too. If direction reverses, the sign of momentum reverses too (e.g. momentum going up is positive, coming down is negative).

Activity — Force, mass and acceleration (trolley and rubber bands)

Textbook · p.93–94

See Newton's second law: how the acceleration depends on the force and on the mass.

🧰 You need
  • Three identical rubber bands
  • A trolley and a smooth, level table
  • Some extra masses to load the trolley
📝 Steps
  1. Put the trolley on the level table and hold it with a string. Attach one rubber band to the trolley (Fig. 4.6).
  2. Stretch the rubber band to a fixed length and release the trolley. Keep moving your hand so the stretch stays the same, and watch the trolley accelerate.
  3. Repeat with two rubber bands side by side, stretched by the same amount, and then with three. Compare the acceleration each time (same mass, bigger force).
  4. Now put extra mass on the trolley and pull with one rubber band, stretched by the same amount as before. Compare the acceleration (same force, bigger mass).
💡 What should you find? (tap)
With more rubber bands (a bigger force) the acceleration is bigger. With more mass on the trolley the acceleration is smaller. So a is proportional to F, and a is inversely proportional to m, which gives a = F / m.

Activity — The balloon rocket (third law)

Textbook · p.96

See Newton's third law: action and reaction.

🧰 You need
  • A balloon filled with air
  • A piece of cellophane tape and a piece of straw (drinking straw)
  • A long thread (string)
📝 Steps
  1. Thread the string through the straw and stretch the string horizontally, fixed at both ends (Fig. 4.8).
  2. Blow up the balloon, keep its mouth closed, and tape it to the straw.
  3. Release the mouth of the balloon, so that the air rushes out. Watch the balloon.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The air rushes out one way (the action) and the balloon moves along the string the opposite way (the reaction). Action and reaction are equal and opposite, and they act on two different objects: air and balloon.

Activity — Two friends pushing on skateboards

Textbook · p.97

See action and reaction when two people push each other.

🧰 You need
  • Two small wooden boards on wheels (like skateboards)
  • Two students, one to sit on each board
📝 Steps
  1. Place the two boards close to each other, and let one student sit on each board (Fig. 4.9).
  2. The two students put their palms against each other and push.
  3. Watch which way each board moves.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
Both students are pushed apart in opposite directions. When one pushes the other, the other pushes back with an equal and opposite force.

Worked Example 2 · F = ma · Ch.4, p.95
A body of mass 6 kg, moving at a steady velocity, has an extra 12 N force applied in its direction of motion. Find the acceleration produced.

Rearrange F = ma to make a the subject: a = F/m

Substitute the values: a = 12 N ÷ 6 kg

= 2 ms-2
Where marks are lost: not showing that dividing N by kg correctly gives the unit ms-2.

A certain object gets an acceleration of 2 ms-2 when an extra force of 8 N is applied in its direction of motion. Find the object's mass.

Substitute the values: m = 8 N ÷ 2 ms-2

= 4 kg
Final answer: Mass = 4 kg.

Formula: momentum = mv

= 10 kg ms-1

Chapter Summary & Key Terms

One last read before the exam!

📌 Summary — 4 key points

  1. Newton's first law: a body stays at rest, or keeps moving at uniform velocity, until an unbalanced force acts on it.
  2. Newton's second law: the acceleration of a body is directly proportional to the unbalanced force applied (F) and inversely proportional to its mass (m).
  3. Newton's third law: for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction.
  4. The weight of an object is the force with which the Earth pulls it towards its centre. It is the force that gives the object the acceleration due to gravity (g): W = m g.
