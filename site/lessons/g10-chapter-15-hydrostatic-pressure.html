Pressure of Liquids

15.1  Pressure: force on each square metre

Press your palm flat on a table, then press the sharp tip of a pencil with the same force. The pencil tip hurts, the palm does not. The force is the same, but the tip has a much smaller area. That idea is called pressure.

What pressure means
Pressure is the force acting on a unit area.
Pressure
Pressure = F ÷ A   (F = perpendicular force, A = area)
The force must act at right angles to the surface.
A box of weight F presses on the table through its contact area A.

The unit of pressure is newton per square metre (N m-2). To honour the French scientist Blaise Pascal it is called the pascal (Pa): 1 N m-2 = 1 Pa. Pressure has only a size and no direction, so it is a scalar.

Example 1
A cubic box of weight 400 N sits on a table. Its bottom area is 0.2 m². Find the pressure on the table.
Pressure = F ÷ A = 400 N ÷ 0.2 m² = 2000 Pa
Example 2
A pile of soil spread over 8 m² of ground exerts a pressure of 150 Pa. What is the force on the ground?
Force = pressure × area = 150 N m-2 × 8 m² = 1200 N

15.2  Pressure in a liquid (hydrostatic pressure)

A solid presses on the table because its weight spreads over the contact area. In the same way a liquid presses on the bottom of its container, and on the vertical walls too. Let us see what this hydrostatic pressure is like.

🔬 Three quick experiments
  • A polythene bag with holes, filled with water: water leaves through every hole, whichever way the hole faces. So the pressure acts in every direction.
  • A bottle with holes at the same level near the bottom: every stream travels the same distance. So the pressure at the same level is the same.
  • A bottle with holes from top to bottom: the lower holes shoot the water faster. So the pressure increases with depth.
Characteristics of liquid pressure
(i) It increases with the height (depth) of the liquid column above the point. (ii) It is the same at the same level of a liquid. (iii) At a point it is the same in all directions. (iv) It depends on the vertical height of the column, not on the shape of the column or the amount of liquid.
Activity 15.1: tubes of different shapes joined at the bottom. The water reaches the same height in all of them.

Take a column of liquid of height h and density ρ standing on a base of area 1 m². Its mass is ρ × (1 × h) = hρ, so its weight is hρg. This weight spreads over 1 m², so the pressure at the bottom is hρg. The same works at any depth inside the liquid.

Pressure due to a liquid column
P = h ρ g
h in m, ρ in kg m-3, g in m s-2 → P in N m-2 (Pa)
The pressure at a point depends on the depth h below the surface.
Example 1
A point in a lake is 1.5 m deep. Find the pressure of the water there. (ρ = 1000 kg m-3, g = 10 m s-2)
P = hρg = 1.5 × 1000 × 10 = 15 000 Pa
Example 2
The sea is 10 m deep at a place. Find the pressure of the sea water there. (ρ = 1050 kg m-3)
P = hρg = 10 × 1050 × 10 = 105 000 Pa

15.3  Pressure travels through a liquid

A liquid cannot be squeezed into a smaller volume. So a pressure made at one point of a liquid is passed on to every other point. Machines built on this idea are called hydraulic machines.

Hydraulic press: a small force on the small piston A gives a large force on the large piston B.
Hydraulic press (textbook figure 15.6)
Piston A has an area of 10 cm² and piston B has 200 cm². A force of 20 N pushes on A.
Pressure = F ÷ A = 20 N ÷ 10-3 m² = 20 000 N m-2 = 2 N cm-2
This same pressure acts on piston B: 2 N on every cm². Total force on B = 2 × 200 = 400 N.

The liquid's own weight is tiny compared with these forces, so it is ignored in the calculation.

  • Vehicle hoist at a service station: a small force on a small piston is transmitted through oil to a large piston carrying the vehicle. (Real hoists use a compressor to pump oil, because moving a small piston a long way is hard.)
  • Hydraulic jack: lifts one side of a vehicle when a wheel must be removed. A force on a small piston lifts a large piston.
  • Vehicle brakes: the pedal pushes the piston of the master cylinder; the oil carries the pressure to the slave cylinder at the wheel. The slave cylinder is wider, so it presses the brake pads harder than the driver pushes the pedal.

15.4  Gases and the atmosphere

Gases press too. A gas can make pressure in two ways: by the weight of a column of gas (this is how atmospheric pressure comes about), or when a compressed gas tries to expand.

🎈 Activity 15.2: a balloon and a U-tube
  • Water in a U-tube stands at the same level in both arms. Connect a balloon full of air to arm X and open it: the water in X goes down and in Y goes up.
  • So the compressed air in the balloon makes extra pressure on the water in X.

The atmosphere is the layer of air around the Earth, hundreds of kilometres thick. Just as water presses on a point at the bottom of a pond, the weight of the air above any point makes the atmospheric pressure.

Torricelli's mercury barometer: the mercury column is held up by the atmospheric pressure.
  1. Fill a glass tube (about 1 m long, closed at one end) with mercury.
  2. Turn it upside down without letting air in, and stand it in a dish of mercury.
  3. The mercury drops a little and stops with a column about 76 cm high. Above it is empty space: a vacuum.

The atmospheric pressure outside pushes on the dish and balances the 76 cm column, so the height of the column measures the atmospheric pressure. Push the tube deeper or tilt it: the vertical height stays 76 cm. Going up a mountain the air column gets shorter, so the pressure falls (about 25 cm Hg on top of Everest).

Atmospheric pressure at sea level
About 76 cm Hg: the pressure of a mercury column 76 cm high. In pascals: P = hρg.
Converting 76 cm Hg
(ρ of mercury = 13 600 kg m-3, g = 10 m s-2, ρ of water = 1000 kg m-3)
(i) P = hρg = (76 ÷ 100) × 13 600 × 10 = 103 360 Pa
(ii) A water column of height h gives the same pressure: h × 1000 × 10 = 103 360, so h = 10.336 m.

Two instruments measure atmospheric pressure: the mercury barometer above, and the aneroid barometer, which has no liquid. It has a sealed metal box with the air pumped out; when the outside pressure changes the thin walls bend, and a pointer moves over a scale.

  • Drinking with a straw: sucking lowers the pressure inside the straw; the atmospheric pressure on the juice outside pushes it up the straw.
  • Siphon: fill a tube with water, lower one end below the level of tank A. The pressure at the end in A (water column + atmosphere) is higher than the pressure at the free end, so water flows to the lower tank B.
  • Rubber sucker: pressed on glass, it pushes out most of the air. The pressure inside is lower than outside, so the atmosphere holds it on (only if no air leaks in at the edge).

15.5  Floating and sinking: upthrust

A stone sinks but a piece of wood floats. Push a floating plank down and you feel the water pushing back up. Even a sinking object feels lighter in water than in air. The upward force that a fluid exerts on an object in it is called the upthrust.

Activity 15.3: the spring balance reads less when the metal is in water, because the water pushes it up.
Stage (Activity 15.4, sample readings)Spring balance (N)Upthrust (N)Weight of displaced water (N)
(a) cube near the surface1.200
(b) half submerged0.90.30.3
(c) fully submerged, near the surface0.60.60.6
(d) fully submerged, deep down0.60.60.6
Archimedes' principle
When an object is partly or completely submerged in a fluid, the upthrust on it equals the weight of the fluid it displaces.
Activity 15.5: A floats partly submerged, B floats fully submerged, C sinks.

In the activity the object that floats (partly or fully submerged) has an upthrust equal to its weight. The object that sinks has a weight greater than the upthrust.

⚖️ When an object is fully immersed in a fluid, the upthrust is…
  • less than its weight → it sinks.
  • equal to its weight → it floats fully submerged.
  • greater than its weight → it rises and floats partly submerged, until the upthrust equals its weight.

A hydrometer is a glass instrument with a bulb loaded with mercury or lead shot, so it floats upright. It sinks only a little in a dense liquid (little liquid has to be displaced) and more in a light liquid. The density is read straight from the scale.

Hands-on Activities

The textbook activities: try them at home or in class
💡 No apparatus?
  • Don't worry: you can see the same ideas in the lab below. But doing it for real makes it stick even better!

Demonstrations: bag and bottle with holes

Textbook · p.64–65

See that water pressure acts in every direction, is the same at the same level, and grows with depth.

🧰 You need
  • A polythene bag
  • A plastic bottle about 25 cm high
  • A pin or nail to make holes, water
📝 Steps
  1. Make holes on different sides of a polythene bag, fill it with water and hold it up.
  2. Make holes at the same level near the bottom of the bottle, fill it with water and watch how far each stream goes.
  3. Make a second bottle with holes from top to bottom, hold it above the ground and compare the streams.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
Water leaves every hole in the bag (pressure in every direction). Streams from holes at the same level travel the same distance (same pressure at the same level). Lower holes shoot water faster (pressure increases with depth).

Activity 15.1: does the shape of the liquid column matter?

Textbook · p.66

Find out whether liquid pressure depends on the shape of the column.

🧰 You need
  • Five transparent tubes of different shapes (a to e)
  • A PVC tube with closed ends
  • Water
📝 Steps
  1. Fix the five tubes to the PVC tube as in the figure.
  2. Fill the system with water.
  3. Record the vertical height of the water column in each tube.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The height is the same in every tube. The pressure at equal levels is equal, so the pressure of a liquid column depends only on its height, not on the amount of liquid or the shape of the column.

Activity 15.2: a compressed gas makes pressure

Textbook · p.71–72

Show that compressed air in a balloon makes a pressure.

🧰 You need
  • A U-tube, water
  • An air-filled balloon, thread
📝 Steps
  1. Pour water into the U-tube until the levels in arms X and Y are equal.
  2. Tie the balloon mouth with a thread (easy to untie). Connect it to arm X with another thread.
  3. Slowly undo the knot on the balloon and watch the water levels.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The water level in X goes down and in Y goes up. The compressed air in the balloon adds pressure on the water in X.

Activity 15.3: showing the upthrust

Textbook · p.77

Show that water pushes an object upwards.

🧰 You need
  • A spring balance, a piece of metal
  • A beaker of water
📝 Steps
  1. Hang the metal on the spring balance and read its weight.
  2. Push the metal down a little: the reading goes up. Pull it up a little: the reading goes down.
  3. Now lower the metal into the water and read the balance.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
A downward force raises the reading and an upward force lowers it. In water the reading falls, so the water exerts an upward force: the upthrust.

Activity 15.4: upthrust and displaced water (Archimedes)

Textbook · p.78–79

Compare the upthrust with the weight of the water the metal pushes out of the way.

🧰 You need
  • A metal cube with a line marking half its volume
  • Spring balance, beaker, water
📝 Steps
  1. Weigh the cube in air, and weigh the empty beaker.
  2. Lower the cube: just at the surface, half submerged, fully submerged near the surface, fully submerged deep down.
  3. Each time, record the balance reading and the weight of the beaker with the displaced water. Upthrust = weight in air − reading.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
Upthrust equals the weight of the displaced water (0.3 N and 0.3 N when half submerged, 0.6 N and 0.6 N when fully submerged). It does not change with depth once fully submerged. This is Archimedes' principle.

Activity 15.5: floating and sinking objects

Textbook · p.80–82

Find how weight and upthrust compare for a floating object and a sinking object.

🧰 You need
  • A: an object that floats partly submerged
  • B: a closed bottle with sand that floats fully submerged
  • C: an object that sinks
  • Spring balance, water
📝 Steps
  1. Measure the weight of A, B and C in air.
  2. Measure their apparent weights in water (A floating, B floating fully submerged, C sunk).
  3. Upthrust = weight − apparent weight. Press A down: do you feel an extra upward force?
💡 What should you find? (tap)
Sample results: A (1.1 N) and B (1.8 N) have an upthrust equal to their weights (1.1 N and 1.8 N). C weighs 2.4 N but the upthrust is only 1.9 N, so it sinks. Press A down and the upthrust becomes larger than its weight, so it springs back to the floating position.

Worked Examples

Step-by-step solutions to the textbook examples
Textbook example · Ch.15, p.63
A cubic box of weight 400 N is on a table. The area of its bottom is 0.2 m². Find the pressure on the table under the box.

Pressure is force on each unit area, so Pressure = force ÷ area. The force is the box's weight, 400 N.

Put the numbers in: 400 N ÷ 0.2 m².

400 ÷ 0.2 = 2000. The unit is N m⁻², which is the pascal.

Final answer: the pressure is 2000 Pa.
Textbook example · Ch.15, p.64
A pile of soil spread over 8 m² of ground makes a pressure of 150 Pa. What force does the soil exert on the ground?

From Pressure = Force ÷ Area we get Force = Pressure × Area.

Force = 150 N m⁻² × 8 m².

150 × 8 = 1200.

Final answer: the force is 1200 N.
Textbook example · Ch.15, p.67
A point in a lake is 1.5 m deep. Find the pressure of the water there. (density of water = 1000 kg m⁻³, g = 10 m s⁻²)

For a liquid column we use P = h ρ g.

h = 1.5 m, ρ = 1000 kg m⁻³, g = 10 m s⁻².

P = 1.5 × 1000 × 10 = 15 000.

Final answer: the pressure is 15 000 Pa.
Textbook example · Ch.15, p.68
At one place the sea is 10 m deep. Find the pressure of the sea water there. (density of sea water = 1050 kg m⁻³, g = 10 m s⁻²)

Again P = h ρ g.

P = 10 m × 1050 kg m⁻³ × 10 m s⁻².

10 × 1050 = 10 500, and × 10 = 105 000.

Final answer: 105 000 Pa. The sea water is denser than lake water, so the pressure is larger.
Textbook example · Ch.15, p.68–69
Piston A of a hydraulic press has an area of 10 cm² and piston B has 200 cm². A force of 20 N is applied on A. What force does B give?

The pressure made by A is Force ÷ Area = 20 N ÷ 10 cm² = 2 N cm⁻².

Liquids pass pressure on without change, so the pressure at B is also 2 N cm⁻²: 2 N on every cm² of B.

Force on B = pressure × area = 2 × 200 = 400 N.

Final answer: 400 N: a 20 N push becomes a 400 N push (20 times bigger, because B is 20 times larger in area).
Textbook example · Ch.15, p.76
The atmospheric pressure at sea level is 76 cm Hg. (ρ of mercury = 13 600 kg m⁻³, g = 10 m s⁻²) (i) Find it in pascals. (ii) What height of water column does it balance? (ρ of water = 1000 kg m⁻³)

(i) Use P = hρg with h = 76 cm = 0.76 m. P = 0.76 × 13 600 × 10.

0.76 × 13 600 = 10 336, and × 10 = 103 360 Pa.

(ii) For water: h × 1000 × 10 = 103 360, so h = 103 360 ÷ 10 000 = 10.336 m.

Final answer: (i) 103 360 Pa, (ii) 10.336 m. That is why a mercury barometer is short and a water barometer would be over 10 m tall!
Textbook activity 15.4 · Ch.15, p.78–79
In Activity 15.4 the cube weighs 1.2 N in air. Half submerged, the balance reads 0.9 N and the beaker with displaced water weighs 1.6 N (the empty beaker weighs 1.3 N). Compare the upthrust with the weight of the displaced water.

Upthrust = weight in air − balance reading = 1.2 − 0.9 = 0.3 N.

Weight of displaced water = beaker with water − empty beaker = 1.6 − 1.3 = 0.3 N.

The two are equal. The same happens when the cube is fully submerged: 0.6 N and 0.6 N.

Final answer: upthrust = weight of the displaced water = 0.3 N (Archimedes' principle).

Every practice question from the textbook, each with a worked solution

Exercise 15.1Pressure and hydrostatic pressure · p.71

1.The pressure at the bottom of a container due to the liquid in it is 1500 Pa. What is meant by "the pressure is 1500 Pa"?
A force of 1500 N acts on every 1 m² of the bottom, because 1 Pa = 1 N m-2.
2.Find the pressure of a mercury column 10 cm high. (density of mercury = 13 600 kg m⁻³)
h = 10 cm = 0.1 m, g = 10 m s-2
P = hρg = 0.1 × 13 600 × 10 = 13 600 Pa
3.The depth of a pond is 1.5 m. Calculate the pressure caused by the water at the bottom.
(ρ of water = 1000 kg m-3, g = 10 m s-2, as in the textbook examples)
P = hρg = 1.5 × 1000 × 10 = 15 000 Pa
4.The depth at a point in the sea is 1 km. Find the pressure of the sea water at the bottom there. (density of sea water = 1050 kg m⁻³)
h = 1 km = 1000 m
P = hρg = 1000 × 1050 × 10 = 10 500 000 Pa (1.05 × 107 Pa)
5(a).A tank of length 5 m, width 3 m and depth 2 m is filled with a liquid of density 800 kg m⁻³. What is the pressure at the bottom of the tank due to the liquid?
P = hρg = 2 × 800 × 10 = 16 000 Pa
5(b).What is the force acting on the bottom of the tank due to that pressure?
Area of the bottom = 5 × 3 = 15 m²
F = P × A = 16 000 × 15 = 240 000 N

Exercise 15.2Applications, atmosphere, upthrust · p.84

1(i).The depth of a reservoir is 1.2 m. Calculate the pressure at the bottom due to the water. (g = 10 m s⁻², density of water = 1000 kg m⁻³)
P = hρg = 1.2 × 1000 × 10 = 12 000 Pa
1(ii).Find the force exerted by the water on an area of 200 cm² at the bottom of the reservoir.
A = 200 cm² = 200 × 10-4 m² = 0.02 m²
F = P × A = 12 000 × 0.02 = 240 N
2(i).Describe a simple experiment to show that "the pressure in a liquid increases with increasing depth".
Take a plastic bottle about 25 cm high. Make equally spaced holes from top to bottom, fill it with water and hold it above the ground. The water from the lower holes leaves with a higher speed and travels farther than from the upper holes. Water leaves faster where the pressure is higher, so the pressure increases with depth.
2(ii).Describe a simple experiment to find out whether the pressure inside a balloon is less than or greater than the atmospheric pressure.
Pour water into a U-tube until both arms are at the same level. Connect the balloon's mouth to one arm (X) and open it. If the water level in X goes down (and rises in the other arm), the pressure in the balloon is greater than the atmospheric pressure. If it goes up, the pressure in the balloon is less.
3(i).The atmospheric pressure at sea level is 76 cm Hg. How much is this in pascals?
(ρ of mercury = 13 600 kg m-3, g = 10 m s-2)
P = hρg = 0.76 × 13 600 × 10 = 103 360 Pa
3(ii).What height of water column exerts the same pressure?
h × 1000 × 10 = 103 360
h = 103 360 ÷ 10 000 = 10.336 m
4(i).Write down Archimedes' principle.
When an object is partly or completely submerged in a fluid, the upthrust acting on it is equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by the object.
4(ii)(a).A piece of metal weighs 20 N in air. Completely immersed in water its apparent weight is 5 N. What is the upthrust exerted on the metal by the water?
Upthrust = weight in air − apparent weight in water
= 20 − 5 = 15 N
4(ii)(b).What is the weight of the water displaced by the piece of metal when it is completely immersed?
By Archimedes' principle the weight of the displaced water equals the upthrust: 15 N.

Chapter Summary & Key Terms

One last read before the exam!

📌 Summary — 9 key points

  1. Pressure is produced by liquids and gases as well as by solids.
  2. The pressure due to a liquid acts in every direction.
  3. The pressure due to a liquid increases as the depth (height of the liquid column) increases.
  4. P = hρg gives the pressure of a liquid column: h = height of the column, ρ = density, g = gravitational acceleration.
  5. The air around the Earth is the atmosphere, and the pressure made by it is the atmospheric pressure.
  6. At sea level the average atmospheric pressure is 76 cm Hg: the pressure of a mercury column 76 cm high.
  7. The mercury barometer and the aneroid barometer measure atmospheric pressure.
  8. An object partly or fully submerged in a liquid feels an upthrust equal to the weight of the liquid it displaces.
  9. An object floating on a liquid: the weight of the liquid displaced equals the weight of the object.
