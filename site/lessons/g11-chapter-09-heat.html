9.1  Temperature and its scales

Temperature tells how hot or cold an object is. It is a measure of the mean kinetic energy of the particles of the object. Touch is not accurate and gives no number, so we use a thermometer (the first was made by Galileo Galilei around 1600 A.D.).

  • Glass-mercury thermometer: mercury in a bulb expands up a narrow tube; the scale is read from the length of the column. Mercury expands uniformly, conducts heat well and is liquid from −39 °C to 357 °C, but it is toxic, so these are used less now.
  • Glass-alcohol thermometer: ethanol (coloured) instead of mercury. It melts at −115 °C, so it suits very low temperatures, and expands a lot and uniformly.
  • Digital thermometer: uses an electrical property, such as resistance, that depends on temperature.
Figures 9.6 and 9.7: the three temperature scales.
  • Celsius scale: the melting point of pure ice is 0 °C and the boiling point of water is 100 °C (at one atmosphere); these fixed points are divided into 100 divisions.
  • Fahrenheit scale: ice melts at 32 °F and water boils at 212 °F: 180 divisions.
  • Kelvin scale: Lord Kelvin showed there is a lowest possible temperature, absolute zero (−273.15 °C), when the particles have no kinetic energy. 0 K is absolute zero, and 1 K is the same size as 1 °C. The Kelvin is the international unit of temperature.
Converting Celsius and Kelvin
T (K) = θ (°C) + 273    θ (°C) = T (K) − 273
one Kelvin degree equals one Celsius degree; only the zeros differ
Example
(i) Write 50 °C in kelvin. (ii) Write 373 K in °C.
(i) 50 + 273 = 323 K
(ii) 373 − 273 = 100 °C

9.2  Heat and heat transfer

Light a burner under one of two identical vessels of water: its temperature rises, the other stays the same. Something has moved from the flame to the water: heat. Heat is the energy transferred from one object to another because of a temperature difference. It is a form of energy (Count Rumford showed this in 1798; James Joule investigated it from 1840), measured in joules (J); the calorie is also used.

Figure 9.9 (Activity 9.1): hot iron in cold water.
🌡️ Which way does heat flow?
  • Heat flows from the body at the higher temperature to the body at the lower temperature, like water flowing downhill.
  • The cooler body warms up; the hotter body cools down.
  • When both reach the same temperature, there is no more flow: thermal equilibrium.

9.3  Heat capacity and specific heat capacity

Heat equal amounts to water and to coconut oil, or to one beaker of water and a beaker with double the water (Activity 9.2): the temperature rises differ. Different objects need different amounts of heat for the same rise.

  • Heat capacity (C): the heat needed to raise the temperature of an object by one unit. Unit: J K-1 (or J °C-1). It depends on the substance and the mass.
  • Specific heat capacity (c): the heat needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by one degree. Unit: J kg-1 K-1. It depends only on the substance.
Heat capacity
C = m c
C in J K-1, m in kg, c in J kg-1 K-1
Quantity of heat
Q = m c θ
Q in J, m in kg, c in J kg-1 K-1, θ = temperature change in K or °C (same size)

Example 1
How much heat raises the temperature of 2 kg of water by 10 K? (c = 4200)
Q = m c θ = 2 × 4200 × 10 = 84 000 J
Example 2
A 500 g aluminium block is heated from 30 °C to 50 °C. Find the heat needed. (c = 900)
Q = 0.5 × 900 × (50 − 30) = 9000 J
Example 3
20 000 J is given to 2 kg of copper at 30 °C. Find the final temperature. (c = 400)
20 000 = 2 × 400 × θ, so θ = 25 °C. Final temperature = 30 + 25 = 55 °C
Example 4
A copper vessel with 1 kg of water weighs 1.6 kg in total. The water is at 25 °C. Find the heat needed to bring it to the boil. (c water 4200, c copper 400)
Vessel mass = 1.6 − 1.0 = 0.6 kg. Vessel: 0.6 × 400 × 75 = 18 000 J. Water: 1 × 4200 × 75 = 315 000 J. Total = 333 000 J

9.4  Change of state and latent heat

A substance can be a solid, liquid or gas, and heating or cooling changes its state: melting, freezing (solidifying), boiling and condensing are changes of state. The melting point is the temperature at which a heated solid becomes liquid; the freezing point of a substance has the same value. The boiling point is where a liquid forms bubbles inside and becomes vapour. All of them depend on the pressure (usually quoted at 1 atmosphere).

Figures 9.12 and 9.13: the temperature stays constant while the state changes.

Heat ice that is just below 0 °C: its temperature rises to 0 °C. Then, while it melts, the temperature does not rise even though heat is still supplied. The heat is spent working against the attractive forces between the molecules. This hidden heat, absorbed with no temperature change during a change of state, is the latent heat. Once all the ice has become water at 0 °C, extra heat raises the temperature again. When the water reaches 100 °C the same happens again as it turns to steam.

  • Latent heat of fusion: the heat absorbed when ice at 0 °C becomes water at 0 °C. The same amount is released when water freezes.
  • Specific latent heat of fusion of ice: 3.36 × 105 J kg-1: the heat to change 1 kg of ice at 0 °C into water at 0 °C.
  • Specific latent heat of vaporization of water: 2.26 × 106 J kg-1: the heat to change 1 kg of water at 100 °C into steam at 100 °C. Steam gives the same heat out when it condenses.
Heat for a change of state
Q = m L   (L = specific latent heat, J kg-1)
the temperature stays constant while Q is absorbed

Vaporization is turning into gas. It happens by boiling (at the boiling point, with bubbles inside the liquid) or by evaporation (slowly, only at the surface, at any temperature below the boiling point). Both absorb latent heat. Evaporation dries clothes, and sweating cools us: the large latent heat of water takes a lot of heat from the skin.

9.5  Thermal expansion

The increase in length, area or volume of a substance when it is heated is thermal expansion; the decrease on cooling is contraction. Two stuck glasses can be parted by pouring cold water in the inner one and standing the outer one in warm water. An iron ball that just passes through a ring will no longer pass when hot (Activity 9.3).

  • Solids: iron rims are fitted to wooden cart wheels while hot; railway rails have small gaps; telephone and power cables hang loosely between poles; a tight metal bottle lid opens after being warmed (metal expands more than glass).
  • Liquids: the liquid in mercury and alcohol thermometers expands up the tube (in Activity 9.4 the level first drops as the glass expands, then rises as the liquid expands more).
  • Gases: warm the air in a bottle and the balloon on its mouth inflates; it shrinks again when the air cools (Activity 9.5).
Figure 9.20: a bimetallic strip.

A bimetallic strip has two metals that expand by different amounts riveted together. When heated it bends, so it can switch off an electric heater when the temperature gets too high. It is used in electric irons and rice cookers to control the temperature.

9.6  Conduction, convection and radiation

Heat is really the random kinetic energy of particles (vibration, rotation, translation). Heat transfer is the spreading of that energy from a hotter region to a colder region, in three ways.

Figure 9.27: conduction along a metal rod.
Conduction
Heat passes along a material from particle to particle by collisions, with no bulk movement of the material. It is the main way heat moves in solids. In metals the free electrons carry energy too, so metals are good conductors.
  • Good conductors: silver, copper, iron, mercury, aluminium. Insulators: wood, plastic, asbestos, clay, wool, air. Water is a very poor conductor.
  • A robin fluffs its feathers to trap air, a poor conductor, so it stays warm. Seals have a thick layer of fat (blubber) to stop heat loss.
Figure 9.28: convection currents.
Convection
When a liquid or gas is heated it expands, becomes less dense and rises; cooler fluid sinks to take its place. This circulation, convection currents, carries heat upwards from the heated part. It only happens in fluids (liquids and gases).

An immersion heater must be fully immersed or placed low: if only the top is heated, the water near the top gets hot and the bottom stays cool, because convection currents do not flow downwards.

Figures 9.31 and 9.32: sea breeze and land breeze.
  • Sea breeze (day): land has a smaller specific heat capacity than sea water, so it heats up faster; the air above it warms, rises and lowers the pressure, and cool air flows from the sea to the land.
  • Land breeze (night): the land cools quickly and the sea slowly, so the air over the sea is warmer and rises; wind blows from the land to the sea.
Figure 9.33: thermal radiation, and surfaces that absorb or reflect it.
Thermal radiation
The flow of heat as electromagnetic waves from a warm body, with no material medium needed. This is how the Sun's heat reaches the Earth through about 150 million kilometres of vacuum. Every hot body radiates.
  • Dark and rough surfaces absorb radiation well. Shiny, polished and white surfaces reflect it well.
  • Cricketers wear white in the sun; people in cold climates wear dark colours indoors; black cooking pots absorb heat fast; the inside of a thermos flask is silvered to reflect heat back or keep it out.
Figure 9.34: a thermos flask uses a vacuum, silvered walls and a stopper.

Hands-on Activities

The textbook activities: try them at home or in class
💡 No apparatus?
  • Don't worry: you can see the same ideas in the lab below. But doing it for real makes it stick even better!

Activity 9.1: hot iron in cold water

Textbook · Activity 9.1, p.7

See heat flow from a hot body to a cold one.

🧰 You need
  • A heated block of iron, a vessel of water at room temperature
  • A thermometer, a stirrer
📝 Steps
  1. Put the hot iron into the cold water.
  2. Stir and watch the temperature of the water.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The water temperature rises and the iron cools until both are at the same temperature (thermal equilibrium). Heat flows from the higher to the lower temperature.

Activity 9.2: different substances, different heating

Textbook · Activity 9.2, p.8–9

Compare the temperature rise of water and coconut oil for the same heat.

🧰 You need
  • Three identical beakers, water, coconut oil
  • Three thermometers, three burners, a stirrer
📝 Steps
  1. Put a measured volume of water in one beaker, the same volume of coconut oil in another and twice that volume of water in the third.
  2. Measure the temperatures. Heat all three for the same time (about 5 minutes).
  3. Measure the temperatures again.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The rises are different although the same heat was supplied: different substances and different masses have different heat capacities.

Activity 9.3: expansion of a solid

Textbook · Activity 9.3, p.17

Show that a metal ball expands when heated.

🧰 You need
  • An iron ball with a holder, a ring through which it just passes
📝 Steps
  1. Check that the cold ball passes through the ring.
  2. Heat the ball and try again.
  3. Cool it and try once more.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The hot ball does not pass through; after cooling it passes again. Solids expand when heated and contract when cooled.

Activity 9.4: expansion of a liquid

Textbook · Activity 9.4, p.20

Watch coloured water expand in a tube.

🧰 You need
  • A test tube with coloured water, a rubber stopper with a glass tube
  • A vessel of warm water
📝 Steps
  1. Fill the test tube, fit the stopper and mark the level A in the glass tube.
  2. Stand the test tube in warm water for a few minutes and watch the level.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The level first drops slightly to B (the glass expands) and then rises to C (the liquid expands more than the glass). Liquids expand when heated.

Activity 9.5: expansion of a gas

Textbook · Activity 9.5, p.21

Show that air expands when warmed.

🧰 You need
  • An empty plastic bottle, a balloon, ice and water, hot water
📝 Steps
  1. Stand the bottle in ice water for a short while, then fit the balloon on its mouth.
  2. Stand the bottle in a vessel and pour in hot water.
  3. Take it out and watch the balloon.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The balloon inflates as the air in the bottle warms and expands, and shrinks when the air cools. Gases expand when heated.

Worked Examples

Step-by-step solutions to the textbook examples
Textbook example · Ch.9, p.5
Write 50 °C in kelvin, and 373 K in degrees Celsius.

To go from Celsius to kelvin, add 273: 50 + 273.

To go from kelvin to Celsius, subtract 273: 373 − 273.

Final answer: 323 K and 100 °C.
Textbook example · Ch.9, p.11
Find the heat needed to raise 2 kg of water by 10 K. (c of water = 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹)

Q = m c θ.

Q = 2 × 4200 × 10.

Final answer: 84 000 J.
Textbook example · Ch.9, p.11
A 500 g aluminium block is heated from 30 °C to 50 °C. Find the heat needed. (c = 900 J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹)

Change the mass to kg: 500 g = 0.5 kg. The temperature change is 50 − 30 = 20 °C.

Q = 0.5 × 900 × 20.

Final answer: 9000 J.
Textbook example · Ch.9, p.11
20 000 J of heat is given to 2 kg of copper at 30 °C. What is the final temperature? (c of copper = 400 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹)

Q = m c θ, so 20 000 = 2 × 400 × θ.

θ = 20 000 ÷ 800 = 25 °C.

Final temperature = 30 + 25.

Final answer: 55 °C.
Textbook example · Ch.9, p.11–12
A copper vessel contains 1 kg of water; the vessel with water has a mass of 1.6 kg. The water is at 25 °C. Find the heat needed to heat the water until it boils. (c of water 4200, c of copper 400 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹)

Both the vessel and the water heat up. Vessel mass = 1.6 − 1.0 = 0.6 kg. The rise is 100 − 25 = 75 K for both.

Vessel: 0.6 × 400 × 75 = 18 000 J. Water: 1 × 4200 × 75 = 315 000 J.

Total = 18 000 + 315 000.

Final answer: 333 000 J.
Practice example · from Q = m L
How much heat is needed to melt 2 kg of ice at 0 °C? (specific latent heat of fusion of ice = 3.36 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹)

The ice is already at its melting point, so all the heat goes into the change of state: Q = m L.

Q = 2 × 3.36 × 10⁵ = 6.72 × 10⁵ J.

Final answer: 672 000 J (the temperature stays at 0 °C).

Exercise 9.2Quantity of heat · p.12

1.Find the heat needed to raise 2 kg of iron from 25 °C to 65 °C. (c of iron = 460 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹)
θ = 65 − 25 = 40 °C
Q = m c θ = 2 × 460 × 40 = 36 800 J
2.Find the temperature of 0.8 kg of aluminium at 30 °C when 14 400 J of heat is transferred to it. (c = 900 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹)
14 400 = 0.8 × 900 × θ = 720 θ
θ = 14 400 ÷ 720 = 20 °C
Final temperature = 30 + 20 = 50 °C
3.A glass vessel of mass 500 g contains 400 g of water at 25 °C. Find the heat needed to boil the water. (c of glass = 840, c of water = 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹)
θ = 100 − 25 = 75 °C for both
Glass: 0.5 × 840 × 75 = 31 500 J
Water: 0.4 × 4200 × 75 = 126 000 J
Total = 31 500 + 126 000 = 157 500 J

Exercise 9.3Heat · p.27–30

1(i).The international unit of temperature is ………… and the international unit of heat is …………
The kelvin (K); the joule (J).
1(ii).The absolute zero is equal to ………… Celsius.
−273 °C (more exactly −273.15 °C).
1(iii).The ………… does not change when absorbing latent heat while the ………… changes.
The temperature does not change while the state changes.
1(iv).The method of transferring heat without the influence of a medium is …………
Radiation (thermal radiation).
1(v).Bodies with low specific heat capacities increase their temperature …………, bodies with high specific heat capacities increase their temperature …………
quickly (fast); slowly.
2(i).Cooling curves of cups A and B are given (graph). What is the temperature of the tea in cup A after five minutes?
Read from the graph: about 55 °C.
(Graph readings are approximate; a few degrees either way is acceptable.)
2(ii).What is the time taken for the tea in cup B to drop by 30 °C?
B starts at about 78 °C and reaches about 48 °C after roughly 4 to 5 minutes.
2(iii).What is the difference in temperature of the tea in the two cups after 15 minutes?
A is at about 28 °C and B at about 24 °C: a difference of about 4 °C.
2(iv).Which cup is made of the material with the lower heat conductivity?
Cup A.
2(v).What is the reason for your answer?
The tea in cup A cools more slowly: its material conducts heat out to the air less quickly, so it loses heat more slowly.
2(vi).What is the ultimate temperature of the tea in the two cups?
The room temperature (about 18 to 20 °C on the graph): heat flows out until the tea is in thermal equilibrium with the room.
3(i).There are two situations that a thermos flask can be used. What are they?
To keep hot liquids hot and to keep cold liquids cold.
3(ii).What techniques are used in the flask to prevent heat loss?
The vacuum between the double walls stops conduction and convection; the silvered walls reflect heat radiation back; the lid (stopper) stops convection and evaporation out of the top.
3(iii).Calculate the heat lost when 500 ml of water at 100 °C cools to 25 °C. (c of water = 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹)
500 ml of water has a mass of 0.5 kg; θ = 100 − 25 = 75 °C
Q = m c θ = 0.5 × 4200 × 75 = 157 500 J
3(iv).It is not appropriate to remove the hot water from a flask and fill it with cold water immediately. What is the reason?
The inner glass wall is hot. Cold water would make it contract suddenly and unevenly (thermal contraction), so the thin glass can crack; the hot wall would also warm up the cold water.
4(i).Find the heat released in cooling 10 g of water at 100 °C down to 25 °C.
m = 10 g = 0.01 kg; θ = 75 °C
Q = 0.01 × 4200 × 75 = 3150 J
4(ii).A burn from steam at 100 °C is more harmful than a burn from boiling water at 100 °C. Explain.
Steam at 100 °C condenses on the skin and gives out its latent heat of vaporization (2.26 × 106 J for every kg) as well as the heat from cooling. Boiling water gives out only the heat from cooling. So steam delivers much more heat to the skin.
5(i).A piece of paraffin is heated and the temperature plotted against time (25 °C at the start, 55 °C flat between 2 and 3 min, 70 °C at 4 min). What is the room temperature?
The graph starts at 25 °C, the room temperature.
5(ii).What is the melting point of paraffin?
The flat part is at 55 °C.
5(iii).How long after starting did the paraffin begin to melt?
After 2 minutes, when the temperature reached 55 °C.
5(iv).Why does the temperature stay constant between 2 and 3 minutes?
The paraffin is melting: the heat supplied is used as latent heat of fusion to break the bonds between the molecules, not to raise the temperature.
5(v).If the heating stops at 4 minutes, sketch how the temperature of the paraffin varies with time afterwards.
The temperature falls, quickly at first and then more slowly, back towards room temperature (25 °C). If it cooled below 55 °C it would go solid again, with a short flat part at 55 °C while it freezes and gives out its latent heat.

Chapter Summary & Key Terms

One last read before the exam!

📌 Summary — 8 key points

  1. Temperature is a measure of the mean kinetic energy of the particles of an object. It is measured with a thermometer in °C, °F or K; the international unit is the kelvin.
  2. Heat is the energy transferred from one object to another because of a temperature difference. Its unit is the joule.
  3. Heat capacity C = m c; the quantity of heat Q = m c θ.
  4. Specific heat capacity c: the heat to raise 1 kg by one degree (J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹).
  5. Latent heat is absorbed or released in a change of state without any change of temperature; its specific value is in J kg⁻¹ (fusion and vaporization).
  6. Thermal expansion is the increase in length, area or volume on heating.
  7. Heat flows from a higher to a lower temperature by conduction, convection and radiation.
  8. Conduction: through particles colliding. Convection: rising of heated fluid. Radiation: electromagnetic waves, no medium.
