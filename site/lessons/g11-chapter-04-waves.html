4.0  What is a wave?

Drop a pebble on still water: a disturbance spreads out in circles. Shake one end of a rope up and down: a disturbance runs along the rope. A disturbance that travels through a medium or through space is a wave.

Figures 4.1 and 4.2: ripples spread, but a floating ball only moves up and down.
Waves carry energy
A wave carries energy from one point to another without carrying the material of the medium with it.

The ball bobs up and down, so energy reached it through the water wave, but the water particles do not travel along with the wave.

  • Waves can travel through a medium: water, a rope, air (sound), liquids and solids.
  • Some waves need no medium: light and heat from the Sun reach the Earth across empty space as electromagnetic waves. Radio waves are electromagnetic waves too.

4.1  Mechanical waves: transverse and longitudinal

Waves that need a material medium are mechanical waves: water waves, sound in air, and the waves on a plucked guitar string. A slinky (a steel coil) lets us study them. Shake a slinky sideways on a table, and a wave runs along it.

Transverse wave: the particles move up and down while the wave moves along.
Transverse waves
The particles of the medium move perpendicular to the direction in which the wave travels. Example: water waves, waves on a string.

In a transverse wave the particles that have moved furthest up are at the crests (A, B in Figure 4.7) and those furthest down are at the troughs (C, D).

Figure 4.9: a longitudinal wave in a slinky.
Longitudinal waves
The particles of the medium oscillate parallel to the direction in which the wave travels. Push the free end of the slinky forward: the coils bunch up (a compression). Pull it back: the coils spread out (a rarefaction). Example: sound waves in air.

4.2  Describing a wave: amplitude, wavelength, period, frequency, speed

We study the simplest waves, sinusoidal waves. A particle in the wave goes from zero displacement to a maximum, back through zero, to a maximum in the other direction and back to zero. That is one oscillation (or vibration).

Figures 4.10 and 4.11: crest, trough, amplitude and wavelength.
  • Amplitude: the maximum displacement of the particles taking part in the wave.
  • Wavelength (λ): the distance between a particle and the closest particle in the same state of motion, for example between two consecutive crests or troughs.
  • Period (T): the time a particle takes for one complete oscillation; also the time the wave takes to travel one wavelength.
  • Frequency (f): the number of oscillations a particle makes in one second. Unit: hertz (Hz), one oscillation per second.
Frequency and period
f = 1 ÷ T
f in hertz (Hz), T in seconds (s)
Wave speed
v = f λ   (v = λ ÷ T)
v in m s-1, f in Hz, λ in m

A wave moves one wavelength λ in one period T, so its speed is v = λ ÷ T. Because f = 1 ÷ T, this is v = f × λ.

4.3  Electromagnetic waves and the spectrum

Electromagnetic waves need no material medium. They are made of an electric field and a magnetic field that oscillate at right angles to each other, and the wave travels at right angles to both. So they are transverse waves.

Figure 4.12: the electric and magnetic fields of an electromagnetic wave.
✅ Characteristics of electromagnetic waves
  • They are not affected by external electric or magnetic fields.
  • They need no material medium.
  • In a vacuum they all travel at 3 × 108 m s-1 (more exactly 2.988 × 108).
Speed of an electromagnetic wave
c = f λ
c = 3 × 108 m s-1 in a vacuum
Figure 4.13: the electromagnetic spectrum (frequencies in Hz).

The different frequency ranges behave differently; together they are the electromagnetic spectrum.

  • Visible light: 4.28 × 1014 to 7.69 × 1014 Hz (wavelengths 690 nm to 400 nm). Violet has the shortest wavelength; then indigo, blue, … up to red: the seven colours of the rainbow.
  • Gamma rays: from radioactive elements; extremely high energy; pass through steel and concrete; destroy living cells, so they treat cancer and sterilise food utensils and surgical instruments.
  • X rays: made by fast electrons hitting a metal target. They pass easily through soft tissue but are absorbed by bones, so they photograph the inside of the body, and are used to check baggage and cargo. Too much exposure can cause cancer.
  • Ultraviolet: "above violet"; bees can see it. Sunlight has a little; it makes vitamin D, but too much causes cataract and skin cancer. Used to kill germs in hospitals and to check hidden marks on banknotes.
  • Infrared ("heat rays"): from hot bodies, felt as warmth; heat photographs, TV remote controls, phone and computer cameras, physiotherapy.
  • Microwaves: radar, mobile phones and microwave ovens. Water and fat in food absorb them and heat up; a magnetron makes them.
  • Radio waves: the longest wavelengths and lowest frequencies; long-distance communication. Information is added by changing the amplitude or the frequency of the wave; antennas send and receive them.

4.4  Sound: how it travels

All sounds are made by vibrating objects: a bee's wings, a cricket rubbing its wings, our vocal cords. The type of energy that gives the sensation of hearing is acoustic energy.

A loud-speaker membrane moves to the right and pushes air molecules together: a compression. It moves left and leaves a rarefaction. These compressions and rarefactions travel forward at the same speed; each air molecule only moves back and forth about its place. So sound is a longitudinal wave.

Figure 4.26: sound needs a medium.

Ring an electric bell inside a bell jar and pump the air out: the sound gets fainter and finally disappears. So sound cannot travel through a vacuum: it is a mechanical wave and needs a medium (unlike light).

MediumSpeed of sound
Dry air at 0 °C≈ 330 m s-1
Dry air at 30 °C≈ 350 m s-1
Water≈ 1400 m s-1
Steel≈ 5000 m s-1

Sound goes faster in warmer air, faster in water than in air (about four times), and fastest in solids. Light is far quicker (3 × 108 m s-1), which is why you see lightning before you hear the thunder.

4.5  Pitch, loudness and quality

Three characteristics of sound let us tell sounds apart: pitch, loudness and quality. Each is a sensation in the ear linked to a property of the wave.

Figures 4.30 and 4.31: waveforms on an oscilloscope.
  • Pitch depends on the frequency. Clamp a hacksaw blade and twang it: lengthen the free end and the frequency and pitch fall. Middle C is 256 Hz and higher C is 512 Hz, twice the pitch.
  • Loudness depends on the energy the wave carries to the ear, which depends on the amplitude. Pluck a string harder: more work, larger amplitude, louder sound.
  • Quality depends on the shape of the waveform. A tuning fork, a violin and a piano playing the same note with the same loudness still sound different, because their waveforms differ.

Pitchfrequency

A regular repeating waveform sounds pleasant (music). An irregular waveform with no repeating pattern is noise.

4.6  Hearing range and ultrasound

The human ear hears frequencies from 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz: the hearing range. Below 20 Hz is infrasound; above 20 000 Hz is ultrasound. The upper limit falls with age. Elephants hear infrasound; dogs hear up to about 40 000 Hz; rabbits, dolphins and bats hear ultrasound.

Bats send out ultrasound and judge the position of obstacles from the echo. Dolphins use it to find small fish, to avoid sharks and to talk to each other.

Figure 4.36: finding the depth of the sea with SONAR.
  • SONAR (Sound Navigation and Ranging): a ship sends an ultrasound pulse down; the depth comes from the time the echo takes to return: distance = speed × time ÷ 2.
  • Medical scans: the heart (size, pulse, blood pumped), the womb and the baby; lithotripsy breaks bladder stones.
  • Flaw detection: ultrasound in a solid does not pass an air gap, so cracks in aircraft parts show up. Also spectacles for blind people and soldering metals.
Example
An ultrasound pulse sent from a ship returns after 4 s. The speed of sound in sea water is 1500 m s-1. How deep is the sea?
Distance there and back = 1500 × 4 = 6000 m. Depth = 6000 ÷ 2 = 3000 m

Hands-on Activities

The textbook activities: try them at home or in class
💡 No apparatus?
  • Don't worry: you can see the same ideas in the lab below. But doing it for real makes it stick even better!

Activity 4.1: a wave in a slinky

Textbook · Activity 4.1, p.74

See a wave travel along a slinky.

🧰 You need
  • A slinky
📝 Steps
  1. Place the slinky on a table.
  2. Hold one end and shake it to the left and right in the plane of the table.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
A wave runs along the slinky. It needs a medium (the slinky), so it is a mechanical wave.

Activity 4.2: transverse waves

Textbook · Activity 4.2, p.75

Find how the particles move in a transverse wave.

🧰 You need
  • A slinky, a few pieces of ribbon
📝 Steps
  1. Tie ribbons at several places on the slinky.
  2. Shake the slinky left and right as in Activity 4.1.
  3. Watch how each ribbon moves compared with the direction of the wave.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The ribbons move sideways while the wave travels along the slinky, at right angles. This is a transverse wave.

Activity 4.3: longitudinal waves

Textbook · Activity 4.3, p.77

Find how the particles move in a longitudinal wave.

🧰 You need
  • A slinky, a ribbon
📝 Steps
  1. Fix one end of the slinky on a table and tie a ribbon to one coil.
  2. Push the free end forward and pull it back, repeatedly.
  3. Watch the coils and the ribbon.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
Pushing forward bunches the coils (compression); pulling back spreads them (rarefaction). The coils move back and forth along the direction of the wave: a longitudinal wave.

Activity 4.4: what decides the pitch?

Textbook · Activity 4.4, p.90

Find how the length of a vibrating blade changes the pitch.

🧰 You need
  • A hacksaw blade, two blocks of wood
📝 Steps
  1. Clamp the blade between the wooden blocks so about 10 cm sticks out.
  2. Make it vibrate and listen.
  3. Increase the free length in steps of 5 cm and listen each time.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
As the free length grows, the frequency falls and the sound gets lower: the pitch depends on the frequency.

Demonstration: a bell in a vacuum

Textbook · Figure 4.26, p.89–90

Show that sound needs a medium.

🧰 You need
  • An electric bell inside a bell jar, a power supply and switch outside
  • A vacuum pump
📝 Steps
  1. Ring the bell inside the jar.
  2. Switch on the vacuum pump to remove the air.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The sound gets fainter and finally stops when the jar is a vacuum: sound cannot travel through a vacuum.

Worked Examples

Step-by-step solutions to the textbook examples
Practice example · from the textbook formula f = 1 ÷ T
A particle in a wave takes 0.02 s for one oscillation. What is the frequency?

Frequency is the reciprocal of the period: f = 1 ÷ T.

f = 1 ÷ 0.02 = 50.

Final answer: 50 Hz.
Practice example · from the textbook formula v = f λ
A wave on a string has a frequency of 5 Hz and a wavelength of 0.4 m. Find its speed.

v = f λ.

v = 5 × 0.4 = 2.

Final answer: 2 m s⁻¹.
Practice example · from c = f λ
A radio station broadcasts at 100 MHz (1 × 10⁸ Hz). Taking c = 3 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, find the wavelength.

c = f λ, so λ = c ÷ f.

λ = 3 × 10⁸ ÷ 1 × 10⁸ = 3.

Final answer: 3 m.
Textbook example · Ch.4, p.97
The time taken by ultrasound waves sent by a ship to return to the detector after reflection from the sea bottom is 4 s. Find the distance between the ship and the sea bottom. (speed of sound in sea water = 1500 m s⁻¹)

The sound travels down and back: distance = 1500 × 4 = 6000 m.

The sea depth is only the one-way distance, half of that: 6000 ÷ 2.

Final answer: 3000 m.
Practice example · thunder and lightning (Exercise 4.1 Q6iii)
You hear the thunder 3 s after seeing the lightning. Taking the speed of sound as 330 m s⁻¹, how far away did the lightning strike? (The light takes almost no time.)

The light arrives almost at once, so the 3 s is the time for the sound to travel.

distance = speed × time = 330 × 3 = 990 m.

Final answer: about 990 m (nearly 1 km).

Practice Exercises

Every practice question from the textbook, each with a worked solution

Exercise 4.1Waves and sound · p.101–102

1(i).A group of children studied the water waves spreading in a still pond when stones were thrown in. What happens to the energy of the waves?
The energy given by the stone is carried outwards by the wave from the point where the stone fell to other places on the water surface.
1(ii).You place a paper boat on the water and disturb the water a small distance away. What change do you see in the boat? What does it show?
The boat moves up and down on the spot but does not travel away with the wave. This shows that the wave transfers energy to the boat, while the water itself does not move along with the wave.
1(iii).Sketch a diagram to show what happens to the water surface.
Concentric circles spread out from the point where the stone fell, and the boat only bobs:
1(iv).Which type of mechanical wave do water waves belong to?
Transverse waves: the water particles move up and down, perpendicular to the direction in which the wave spreads.
1(v).In what way do these waves differ from sound waves travelling in air?
Sound waves in air are longitudinal: the air particles move back and forth along the direction of travel, forming compressions and rarefactions. Water waves are transverse.
2(i).The end B of the metal blade AB is clamped to a table, and the blade is made to vibrate by a force on end A. Sketch one vibration. Use C and D for the maximum displacements.
One vibration: A moves to C (maximum one side), back through the rest position A, to D (maximum other side) and back to A.
2(ii).Describe what is meant by the amplitude using the points A, C and D.
The amplitude is the maximum displacement of the blade from its rest position: the distance from the rest position A to C (or A to D, which is the same).
2(iii).If 50 vibrations take place in 5 seconds, find the frequency of the vibrations of the blade.
f = number of vibrations ÷ time = 50 ÷ 5 = 10 Hz
2(iv).The vibrations give rise to compressions and rarefactions in air. The distance between two consecutive compressions equals which physical quantity of the sound wave?
The wavelength of the sound wave.
2(v)(a).Which sound characteristic depends on the frequency?
Pitch.
2(v)(b).Which sound characteristic depends on the amplitude?
Loudness.
2(v)(c).The same note played on several instruments can be told apart. Which characteristic is this?
The quality of the sound (the shape of the waveform).
3(i).Electromagnetic waves do not need a medium. Write three characteristics of electromagnetic waves.
(1) They are not affected by external electric or magnetic fields. (2) They need no material medium. (3) They travel at 3 × 108 m s-1 in a vacuum. (Also: they are transverse waves.)
3(ii)(a).What is the angle between the electric field and the magnetic field of an electromagnetic wave?
90°: the fields oscillate perpendicular to each other.
3(ii)(b).What is the angle between these two fields and the direction of propagation?
90°: the wave travels perpendicular to both fields.
4(a).The figure shows a segment of a string with a transverse wave. The distance between points D and E equals which physical quantity?
D and E are two consecutive crests: the distance is the wavelength (λ).
4(b).The same distance is shown by the distance between two other points. Which letters?
A and C: they are in the same state of motion, one full wave apart (up to the crest D, down through B, the trough, and back to the axis at C).
5(i).Name (a) two string instruments, (b) two percussion instruments, (c) two wind instruments you expect in the school music room.
(a) violin, guitar (or sitar, banjo, cello). (b) tabla, rabana (or dawula, udekki, thammattama, xylophone, bell). (c) flute, trumpet (or saxophone, clarinet).
5(ii)(a).Write two factors on which the frequency of the sound from a string instrument depends.
Any two of: the length of the vibrating string, the tension of the string, the mass of a unit length of the string.
5(ii)(b).Write two factors on which the frequency of the sound from a wind instrument depends.
The length of the air column (changed on a flute by opening or closing holes). The textbook gives only this one factor; a second factor, which this chapter does not state, is the speed of sound in the air in the instrument (it changes with the temperature).
5(ii)(c).Write two factors on which the frequency of the sound from a percussion instrument depends.
The area and the tension of the vibrating membrane or metal plate.
6(i).Explain scientifically: when a ringing bell is held by hand, it stops ringing.
A bell sounds because its metal vibrates. Holding it stops the vibrations (the hand takes the energy), so no sound waves are made in the air and the sound stops.
6(ii).Explain scientifically: the pitch of a flute changes when the holes are opened one by one.
The pitch of a wind instrument depends on the length of the air column. Opening holes one by one shortens the vibrating air column, so the frequency rises and the pitch becomes higher than when all holes are closed.
6(iii).Explain scientifically: lightning and thunder happen together but we see the light before we hear the thunder.
Light travels at 3 × 108 m s-1, so it reaches the eyes almost instantly, but sound travels only about 330 m s-1, so it takes a longer time to reach the ears.

Chapter Summary & Key Terms

One last read before the exam!

📌 Summary — 9 key points

  1. A wave is a disturbance travelling in a medium or in space.
  2. Waves that need a material medium are mechanical waves.
  3. In transverse waves the particles move perpendicular to the direction of the wave; in longitudinal waves they move parallel to it.
  4. Amplitude, wavelength, period and frequency describe a wave; f = 1 ÷ T and v = f λ.
  5. Electromagnetic waves need no medium; they travel at 3 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ in a vacuum and are transverse.
  6. Sound waves are longitudinal mechanical waves and need a medium.
  7. Pitch depends on frequency, loudness on amplitude, quality on the waveform.
  8. The human hearing range is 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz; below is infrasound, above is ultrasound.
  9. Musical instruments are string, percussion or wind instruments; regular periods sound pleasant, irregular ones are noise.
