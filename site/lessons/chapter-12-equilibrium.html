Equilibrium of Forces

Why doesn't a book on a table fall? Why does a tug-of-war rope stay still when both teams pull equally hard? Why doesn't a child on a swing fall? The answer to all of these is the equilibrium of forces. Deep explanations, lots of examples below.

12.1  What is Equilibrium? — Forces Everywhere, Yet Nothing Moves!

You've watched a tug of war. Both teams pull the rope their own way. If one side pulls harder, the rope moves that way. But if both sides pull with equal force? The rope stays perfectly still! That's because the forces on both sides are balanced — in equilibrium. We say the rope is in equilibrium.

Another example — hang a ball from a strong thread. The ball's weight pulls it down; the thread pulls it up at the same time. Since these two forces are equal, the ball just hangs there — it neither falls nor rises.

What equilibrium means
When the resultant of all the forces acting on an object is zero, the object is in equilibrium.

In real life an object is rarely acted on by just one force. Two, three or more forces may act — and if they balance, the object stays at rest (or keeps moving at a steady speed). In this chapter we'll look at two-force and three-force equilibrium separately. (Remember Newton's first law? With no unbalanced force, rest stays rest. This is exactly that!)

12.2  Equilibrium Under Two Forces

What do we need for an object under just two forces to be in equilibrium? Try this: hang a ring between two spring balances pulled in opposite directions. When the ring is at rest, both readings are equal. Now try keeping the ring still with the two balances NOT on the same straight line — you simply can't!

Three conditions for two-force equilibrium
For two forces to be in equilibrium:
  1. The two forces must be equal in magnitude.
  2. They must act in opposite directions.
  3. They must act along the same straight line.

Imagine pulling a block of wood from both sides with spring balances. Equal pulls — the block stays still. Pull harder on one side, and the block moves toward the stronger pull — because the resultant is no longer zero.

🏠 Examples right at home
  • A book on a table: its weight (W) pulls down; the table pushes up with a normal reaction (R). W = R, opposite, same line — so the book doesn't fall.
  • An object hanging from a rope: weight W down; the rope's tension (T) up. T = W — so the object stays at rest.

12.3  Equilibrium of Three Parallel Forces in One Plane

A bunch of bananas hangs from a light rod by three cords. All three forces are parallel — two pull up, the bananas' weight pulls down. This is a classic example of three parallel forces in equilibrium.

Another one — a child sitting on a swing. Two ropes pull up (F₁, F₂), and the child's weight (W) pulls down. These three forces balance, so the child stays at rest. Here, F₁ + F₂ = W.

Three parallel forces
F₁ + F₂ = W (total upward = total downward)
The textbook's metre-rule experiment shows the same: the two spring balance readings add up to the rule's weight.
For three parallel forces to be in equilibrium
  1. All three forces must lie in one plane.
  2. One force must act opposite to the other two.
  3. The resultant of two of the forces must equal the third in magnitude and be opposite in direction.

12.4  Equilibrium of Three Non-Parallel Forces in One Plane

You've seen a picture hung on a wall with two slanting cords. The cord tensions F₁ and F₂ are slanted (not parallel), and the picture's weight W pulls down. These three forces balance, so the picture stays still.

Hang a thin plate by a thread from three different points, and each time mark the vertical line of the thread on the plate. All three lines meet at one point — the plate's centre of gravity! This shows that for three slanting forces in equilibrium, their lines of action always meet at a single point.

For three non-parallel forces to be in equilibrium
  1. All three forces must lie in one plane.
  2. Their lines of action must meet at a single point.
  3. The resultant of two must be equal to the third, in the opposite direction.

The same idea works for more than three forces — hang a board by four ropes at its corners and the four upward tensions together equal the board's weight, so it stays at rest.

⚠️ Common mistakes
  • You need all three conditions! For two forces, equal size alone isn't enough — they must also be opposite and along the same line.
  • Equilibrium doesn't only mean "not moving". Zero resultant means the object is either at rest or moving at a steady speed.
  • Unequal forces mean no equilibrium. With 20 N and 25 N opposed, there's a 5 N net force — the object moves toward the bigger pull.
  • For three slanting forces, the lines must meet at one point. Even if they're in one plane, no common meeting point means no equilibrium.

Hands-on Activities

The textbook's experiments — try them at home or in class

Activity 1 — Two forces on a ring

Textbook · p.213

Find what is needed for two forces to keep a ring at rest.

🧰 You need
  • A ring
  • Two newton balances (spring balances)
📝 Steps
  1. Place the ring on a table and pull it with the two spring balances in opposite directions with different forces, trying to keep the ring at rest (Fig. 12.4).
  2. Each time the ring is at rest, read both balances.
  3. Now try to keep the ring at rest with the two balances NOT on one straight line. Can you?
💡 What should you find? (tap)
When the ring is at rest, the two readings are equal, the forces are opposite, and they act along one straight line. You cannot keep the ring at rest if the two forces are not on the same line.

Activity 2 — Two forces on a block

Textbook · p.213–214

See what happens to a block when it is pulled from both sides.

🧰 You need
  • A regular wooden block
  • Two newton balances
  • Two rings to attach the balances to the block
📝 Steps
  1. Fix a ring at the middle of each end of the block (Fig. 12.5).
  2. Attach a spring balance to each ring and pull the block to both sides with different forces.
  3. Watch the block, and read both balances each time.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
If the two forces are different, there is a resultant and the block moves in the direction of the larger force. When the resultant is zero, the two forces are equal and the block stays at rest.

Activity 3 — Three parallel forces (metre rule)

Textbook · p.216

Find the relationship between the three parallel forces on a hanging metre rule.

🧰 You need
  • A metre rule
  • Two newton balances
  • Rubber bands to hang the rule
📝 Steps
  1. Find the weight W of the metre rule.
  2. Hang the rule horizontally from the two spring balances using rubber bands, and read the two balances, F₁ and F₂ (Fig. 12.10).
  3. Add F₁ + F₂ and compare it with W.
  4. Try to keep the rule in equilibrium by also pushing one end at right angles to the rule, and by changing the plane of the forces. Notice when it works.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
The sum of the two balance readings equals the weight of the rule: F₁ + F₂ = W. The rule stays in equilibrium only when the three forces lie in one plane, one force acts opposite to the other two, and the resultant of two equals the third.

Activity 4 — Three non-parallel forces (thin plate)

Textbook · p.218

Show that the lines of action of three non-parallel forces in equilibrium meet at one point.

🧰 You need
  • A thin plate (e.g. cardboard)
  • Strings
📝 Steps
  1. Hang the thin plate from three different places in turn, using a string.
  2. Each time, mark on the plate the line the string makes, continued across the plate.
  3. Mark the point where the three lines meet and take it as the centre of gravity of the plate.
  4. Hold the plate in equilibrium with two strings and mark the lines of the two strings and the vertical line through the centre of gravity.
💡 What should you find? (tap)
All three lines meet at one point. For three non-parallel forces in equilibrium, the forces must lie in one plane, their lines of action must meet at a point, and the resultant of any two must equal the third in the opposite direction.

Concept Walkthroughs

Step-by-step reasoning for real textbook questions
Textbook Question · Ch.12, p.221
A box rests on a table. What is the reason the box stays at rest?

First, list the forces on the box: (1) its weight W — downward; (2) the table's normal reaction R — upward.

Now check all three conditions: W = R (equal) ✓, one up and one down (opposite) ✓, both along the same line ✓.

Key idea: the resultant is zero, so the box stays at rest.
Final answer: The box's weight (down) equals the table's normal reaction (up), opposite and along the same line — so the resultant is zero and the box is in equilibrium at rest.
Textbook Question · Ch.12, p.220
An object on a horizontal surface is pulled with 20 N in one direction. (i) What force must be applied in the opposite direction to bring it to rest? (ii) What happens if 25 N is applied in the opposite direction instead?

(i) At rest = equilibrium = zero resultant. Two-force equilibrium needs equal size, opposite direction, same line. So apply 20 N in the opposite direction along the same line.

(ii) Now 25 N opposes 20 N. Resultant = 25 − 20 = 5 N, in the direction of the 25 N force. It isn't zero — so no equilibrium.

Key idea: with an unbalanced 5 N force, the object starts moving in the direction of the 25 N force.
Final answer: (i) 20 N in the opposite direction. (ii) Net force 5 N toward the 25 N force — the object moves that way.
Textbook Question · Ch.12, p.220
Of three slanting forces acting on a ring, if the resultant of B and C is known, what must be done to hold the ring at rest?

Recall the rule for three slanting forces: the resultant of two must equal the third, in the opposite direction.

The resultant of B and C is known. So apply the third force A equal in size to that resultant, in the opposite direction, along a line through the same point.

Remember: the three lines of action must meet at one point.
Final answer: Apply a force (A) equal in size to the resultant of B and C, in the opposite direction, at the same point — the ring will be in equilibrium at rest.

Practice Exercises

Every practice question from the textbook — each with a full worked solution

Chapter ExerciseCh. 12, p.220–221

1(i).An object on a horizontal surface is pulled with 20 N in one direction. What force must be applied in the opposite direction to bring it to rest?
A force of 20 N in the opposite direction, along the same line (equal size, opposite direction, same line).
1(ii).What happens if a 25 N force is applied in the opposite direction instead?
25 − 20 = 5 N
Net force is 5 N toward the 25 N force — the object moves that way (no equilibrium).
2.A vehicle with its engine off is to be moved a short distance. How should forces be applied to it?
To move it you need an unbalanced force (non-zero resultant): push or pull in the desired direction with a force larger than the resisting forces such as friction.
3.Three non-parallel forces act on a ring. If the resultant of B and C is known, what must be done to keep the ring at rest?
Apply the third force A equal in size to the resultant of B and C and opposite in direction, so the three lines meet at one point (see Concept Walkthrough 3).
4.A box is placed on a table. What is the reason it stays at rest?
The box's weight (W) acts downward; the table's normal reaction (R) acts upward. They are equal, opposite and along the same line — resultant zero, so the box stays at rest.
5.An object on a horizontal table is pulled to both sides by two cords with two different forces. What can you say about the nature of its motion?
Because the forces differ, the resultant is not zero — it equals the difference, in the direction of the larger force. So the object is not in equilibrium; it moves (accelerates) toward the larger pull. Only if the forces were equal would it stay at rest.

⭐ Must-know cheat sheet

  • equilibrium: the resultant of all the forces is zero
  • two forces: equal size, opposite direction, same line (R = W, T = W)
  • three parallel forces in one plane: F₁ + F₂ = W

Chapter Summary & Key Terms

One last read before the exam!

📌 Summary — 5 key points

  1. If two forces acting on an object in one plane are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction (along one line), they are in equilibrium and the object is at rest.
  2. For three parallel forces, if the resultant of two is equal to the third and acts in the opposite direction, the three forces are in equilibrium. Example: 20 N balanced by two 10 N forces on the other side.
  3. For three non-parallel forces in one plane, if the resultant of any two is opposite to the third, the three forces are in equilibrium, and their lines of action meet at a point.
  4. Applying a force C opposite to the resultant of A and B (of equal size) puts the three forces A, B and C in equilibrium.
  5. When more than three forces act, they can still be balanced by applying suitable forces.
